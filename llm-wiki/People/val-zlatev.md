Val Zlatev is a portfolio manager at Diamond Hill Capital Management, the Columbus, Ohio-based asset manager, where he joined as a research analyst in the early 2010s and now co-manages the firm's large-cap equity strategies. His beat is technology and semiconductors — he has discussed memory-chip makers Micron, Samsung, and SK Hynix in financial media and on investing podcasts — and his style of argument is bottoms-up and specific: head counts, supply chains, and deal math rather than narrative.

His strongest documented call comes from a single appearance on June 20, 2026, on The Monetary Matters Network, sharing an episode pointedly titled "Accounting Mismatch in AI Profits" with veteran short-seller Jim Chanos. Against the bear case that AI profits are an accounting mirage, Zlatev offered a labor-based rebuttal: "look at head counts over the last three or four years compared to the operating profits of these companies," where the divergence between flat staffing and surging profits is, in his telling, evidence of real productivity rather than financial engineering ([Accounting Mismatch in AI Profits | Jim Chanos and Val Zlat…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NlIsoPhQePs)). His structural bull case concentrated in memory, arguing that high-demand memory names trade at "cheap forward multiples" because the physical supply chain constrains how quickly capacity can arrive ([Accounting Mismatch in AI Profits | Jim Chanos and Val Zlat…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NlIsoPhQePs)). The framing is unambiguous about where value sits in the AI stack: "Technology is the ultimate differentiator in that game... the value add is in the chips and the wrappers of the chips" ([Accounting Mismatch in AI Profits | Jim Chanos and Val Zlat…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NlIsoPhQePs)).

What makes the appearance worth studying is that the bullishness is selectively applied rather than wholesale. He conceded that where AI infrastructure deals actually disclose their economics, they are "penciling out at 7%, 6%, 5%, 8%" — thin returns that cut against the buildout's math — and he flagged outright "shorting opportunities on a bunch of component makers" selling into PCs and smartphones, the devices being displaced by the data-center boom ([Accounting Mismatch in AI Profits | Jim Chanos and Val Zlat…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NlIsoPhQePs)). On the three big memory producers — "one of which is American, two of which are Korean" — he acknowledged the stocks "have gone up so much" even while pressing the structural case ([Accounting Mismatch in AI Profits | Jim Chanos and Val Zlat…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NlIsoPhQePs)). He kept two hedges in view: that demand can be tracked in real time through open-routed token counts and GPU rental prices, and that a genuinely new, non-transformer model architecture could scramble the chip stack ([Accounting Mismatch in AI Profits | Jim Chanos and Val Zlat…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NlIsoPhQePs)). His meta-take on the debate itself is notably balanced — "there's probably just as many bears as bulls on the AI argument, which I think is extremely healthy" ([Accounting Mismatch in AI Profits | Jim Chanos and Val Zlat…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=NlIsoPhQePs)).
