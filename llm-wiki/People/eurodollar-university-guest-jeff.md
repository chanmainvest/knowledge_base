Jeff Snider is an American macro analyst whose reputation rests on one stubborn thesis: the global economy doesn't run on the money central banks print — it runs on the offshore dollar-funding network known as the eurodollar system, and when that system tightens, you get structural dollar shortages, deflationary pressure and low growth no matter how much the Fed "prints." He leads macro research at Alhambra Investments and founded Eurodollar University, the research and education platform behind the podcasts and video analysis where he works through household-survey labor data, full-time versus part-time employment, Treasury yields, private credit, oil and bitcoin. The posture is deliberately contrarian to the mainstream "money-printing means inflation" narrative, and his track record in this corpus reflects it: 5 bullish, 6 bearish and 17 neutral tags — far more signal-interpreter than directional tipster.

His sharpest dated call came on June 14, 2026, in [Now That Iran Is Done, The Real Oil Crisis Begins](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=DTh7cPI8z3E). Against the reflex that a supply shock must be inflationary, he argued the opposite: "Energy shocks are not inflationary. They end up destroying demand, especially for an economy that's weak to begin with... They don't end in inflation." His evidence was physical, not financial — "Chinese imports of petroleum absolutely crashed... physical crude imports were down by 3% in March, 20% in April, and 29% in May." He mapped a path rather than shouting a price: the front of the curve would "come down and then bounce," traders would rediscover that "the fundamental value is what we saw in December, not" April's spike, and that's "how you get to $50 a barrel" — while conceding prices weren't snapping straight back down, with "how long they take" the open question.

A week later, on June 21, came [Swiss Bond Market Just Gave A Dire Warning to the World](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=c2LzapdRuT4), where he flagged that "Swiss bond rates are almost back at zero all over again. The critical two-year instrument yielding just a couple of basis points," and that "Chinese rates are similarly ultra and that curve is bull steepening." The stance tracker duly flips both his oil and Chinese-equities views from bearish to bullish on that date — though the supporting quote is about bonds, not barrels, and it arrives under a "dire warning" headline. That tension is worth flagging rather than smoothing over: whatever the tags encode, in Snider's framework the bond market is the tell, and collapsing short-end yields across two major markets are a deflationary signal he treats as dependable — bullish in the sense of conviction about what bonds are saying, not optimism about growth.

By June 27, in [Bitcoin And BlackRock Just Did Something VERY Strange](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=biyCX7UYAfQ), his attention had shifted to late-cycle plumbing. He dismissed a causal link between bitcoin and BlackRock's moves — "it's not Bitcoin leading to BlackRock or BlackRock leading to Bitcoin. It's these common late cycle stage behaviors that are acting on both" — and zeroed in on private-credit stress: "Apollo's fund is now cash flow negative. They have more redemption requests. They blocked off the redemption requests," while stock investors treat BlackRock as "one of the biggest examples of a private credit manager" and head for the exits. He also pushed back on the "strong and resilient" US economy narrative and offered the Hang Seng as his gauge — rising, "a pretty dependable" reflationary signal; falling, the opposite.
