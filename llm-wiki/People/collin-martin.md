Collin Martin is a Managing Director and Fixed Income Strategist at the Schwab Center for Financial Research, where — as a CFA charterholder — he covers the bond complex end to end: U.S. Treasuries, investment-grade and high-yield corporate debt, and municipals, with regular commentary on rate trends, credit conditions, and government and corporate issuance. He's a frequent guest on financial media and podcasts, and in this corpus he appears exactly once: on David Lin's show, in an episode framed around a Fed-driven market sell-off and the question of whether rate hikes are coming back ([Fed Sparks Market Bloodbath: Rate Hikes Coming Back? | Coll…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CcecLrxrhF0)).

What's notable about that appearance is how firmly Martin resists the panic the framing invites. He lays out the bear case on fiscal grounds plainly enough — total debt around $40 trillion, debt-to-GDP above 100%, and "very, very large deficits" in non-recessionary periods — and he worries that too much market intervention, whether via the Treasury General Account or by changing the size of coupon issuance, can backfire ([Fed Sparks Market Bloodbath: Rate Hikes Coming Back? | Coll…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CcecLrxrhF0)). But he also pushes back on the doom narrative from the macro side: the economy is resilient, corporations are posting record earnings, and the labor market is stable ([Fed Sparks Market Bloodbath: Rate Hikes Coming Back? | Coll…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CcecLrxrhF0)). His sharpest analytical point is about transmission: higher long-end yields are "a negative for businesses" because it boosts borrowing costs — and not everyone borrows at the short end ([Fed Sparks Market Bloodbath: Rate Hikes Coming Back? | Coll…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CcecLrxrhF0)).

His strongest calls are range calls rather than directional bets, which is itself telling. He expects the 10-year Treasury to hold in a 4.25% to 4.75% band, and he tracks the 30-year around the 5.2–5.3% area, noting it touched 5.3% before settling roughly 10 basis points lower ([Fed Sparks Market Bloodbath: Rate Hikes Coming Back? | Coll…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CcecLrxrhF0)). The closest thing to an outright stance in the record is on credit, where he says Schwab has held "a more favorable view" on investment grade and high yield "for a few months now" and that it hasn't changed — a relative preference, not a bull call ([Fed Sparks Market Bloodbath: Rate Hikes Coming Back? | Coll…](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=CcecLrxrhF0)).
