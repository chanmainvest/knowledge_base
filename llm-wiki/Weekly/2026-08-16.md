## The digest

If one story swallowed the week, it was the long end of the Treasury curve — rates-and-bonds drew 55 of the week's recorded market views, more than double any other theme, and the trigger was the Treasury's buyback announcement. [Eurodollar University](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=fDNBPn7jmUw) was scathing about the initial program, calling it "a pitiful $2 billion, two and a half billion when you include the tips" and mocking the idea of controlling the curve "with just a couple billion extra buybacks" — yet by the next day [David Lin was covering why Treasury doubled it](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BUariIMBL_A), and [Luke Gromen argued it is only the beginning](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=IsswgLUkUas): 10-year yields "have to go way higher," but Besson won't let them, which means "the dollar's got to get a lot weaker to make hedging costs go down" — and "all roads lead to gold." [Joseph Wang](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KYirz5POgZg), surveying a 30-year at 5.3% ("historically high"), noted there is "no limit to this. He could just really buy it all," and that cutting long-end issuance would signal just as loudly. The eight-guest [$40T question panel](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=SWsGPoxqcR0) put the line in the sand at 5% on the 10-year — below it, "the game of chicken continues" — and warned of "a breaking point where… long-term bonds of Britain or Germany or US don't sell," while [Chris Whalen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BCgHELtTnog) asked plainly when the next Treasury-market crisis hits and how much paper the Fed absorbs. [Peter Boockvar](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UrCuK2K8mAU) said rates are not done surging and a Treasury "bazooka" is coming, with the dollar ending "lower"; against the bears, [George Gammon](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=enxD5EPAFCE) predicted rates will most likely be lower in a year, insisting the long end "is not driven by debt, deficits, or oddly enough, the typical version of supply and demand."

The second shadow over everything was AI-adjacent credit. [Robin Wigglesworth](../People/robin-wigglesworth.md) mapped [$1.5 trillion of hidden debt](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=kZXjQZWUHH0) behind the boom, expecting public BDCs at "30 cents or 40 cents of net asset value" in a crisis and calling it "a bad default cycle. It's started already," with recovery rates penciled in unrealistically. [Patrick Boyle](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wTiYaWFP59Q) supplied the receipts: Fitch's July private-credit default rate at a record high, non-performing loans at the 20 largest listed funds the worst since 2017, one big fund with 7% of its book distressed — and the observation that AI is being built faster than the railway mania, 1920s electrification or dot-com, with manias historically fading around year five and "we're currently in year three." [Eurodollar University](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=O450x8yyZ-k) pushed it onto Nvidia specifically: if defaults trigger collateral clauses, "the equipment gets seized, which means Nvidia has to stand" behind it — framing it as "not about private credit in the same way that the 2008 crisis wasn't really about subprime." The near-term test is Nvidia earnings, which the [MacroVoices desk](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XwI_N0Mvhgs) expected to "beat and have a solid guidance"; [Andrew Kang](../People/andrew-kang-robo-strategies-robo-global-strategies-guest.md) offered the bull echo on the real-economy side, calling it "almost impossible for the robotics market not to explode."

The sharpest head-on fight was over the S&P itself — the week's most-called instrument at 26 calls. On the [Maggie Lake show](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wV4dksg_XjE), Erik of YWR called the earnings picture "insanely good": the $400 EPS he expected for 2028 is arriving in 2027, "put a 25 times on it and that's S&P 10,000," with bank headwinds "flipping to tailwinds" and it being "hard to have a big crisis when the banks haven't been lending since the GFC." [Chris Vermeulen](../People/chris-vermeulen.md) joined the bull side after support held — "now we've got this next leg higher started" — while refusing fixed exit numbers like 8,000. Against them, [Harry Dent](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=TInyOyKDOgI) called for "the biggest reversal in history… bigger than '29 to '32," with the bear camp's scenario a 50%-down S&P by year-end and Dent promising to buy US tech only "when they're down the most in history." The same schism ran through bonds and housing: one camp said TLT "would literally double" in a serious downturn as it did in 2008, the other planned to act if TLT slides toward 80; housing bulls need Treasury to "take the long end down," bears see continued pressure if yields keep rising.

The yen thread ran underneath it all. [Weston Nakamura](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XFNk96CabCg) argued dollar-yen "would have already hit 200 if not for these… intervention caps" and sees 200 long-term, describing an unprecedented divergence — Japanese equities at multi-decade highs while the yen gets "crushed" and the inflationary mindset "broken"; [Chris Whalen](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=BCgHELtTnog) agreed the yen "is going to continue to weaken." Gold threaded through nearly every answer: Darius Dale watched for "legitimate progress back to those April highs near 4,800," while Gammon split his own verdict — gold standard plausible "in the next three years, four years, five years," but reserve-currency loss "at the very least 25 years." Oil near $88 was tied by Wang to "the war in Iran driving up energy prices"; Justin Huhn was "extremely constructive" on uranium with hyperscalers "sniffing around" NexGen; and the mining chatter (CoTec and Silvercorp among the most-discussed tickers) stayed promotional but hedged — pig iron at "$300 to 350" cost but funding "at a significant discount" to a $2.5B valuation, and a "safety shutdown in China" annoying Silvercorp.

On mind-changes: this week's corpus captures no clean, documented reversals, and it's worth saying so plainly rather than manufacturing one. The closest things are recalibrations — Dale declaring the dollar's prior bull market "neutralized" while stopping short of being "an outright bear," and Vermeulen's turn to "next leg higher" language once support held, though the record doesn't show his prior stance to date it against. Dent's pledge to buy tech at maximum pessimism is a conditional, not a conversion.
