## What the corpus says

The corpus for Rates, Bonds & the Dollar is substantial in volume — 27 tickered calls and 107 broad views — but unusually thin in attribution: every entry arrives stripped of speaker names and citation links, so the "key voices" here can only be identified by date and position rather than by name. The tickers themselves tell you where the attention is: the 10-year Treasury yield (^TNX, 13 calls) and the dollar index (DX-Y.NYB, 9), with the yen pairs trailing behind. On rates, the corpus is remarkably unambiguous. On August 3 one participant stated flatly that "I think bond yields are going to move higher," and by August 11 that had hardened into "bond markets suffering. In other words, yields going up." Nobody in the extracted material makes the case for lower yields; the bond market is treated as the pressure point everyone is watching.

The dollar is where the debate turns genuinely combative, and it whipsaws inside a single week. On August 3 the technical read was bearish: "the dollar is breaking an uptrend. We just fell through some support levels right at the 100 101 level." Three days later, on August 6, the same instrument produced the opposite conclusion — "the long-term moving average for the dollar is saying hey it is going up. It has all the characteristics of a bull market." Whether that's two analysts disagreeing or one analyst capitulating and flipping is impossible to tell without names, which is frustrating, because it's the most interesting question in the tape. The yen commentary has the same problem in mirror image: an August 6 call that "the yen would continue going down because the fundamentals point to going down" sits against an August 3 warning that a "strengthening of the yen in yields moving higher should collapse that yen carry trade." The corpus holds both the yen-grinds-lower and the yen-snaps-higher scenarios simultaneously, and the August 7 framing explains why it matters: "if the yen were to weaken substantially, then the other currencies would follow it," because "a stable yen is not only important for the US, but it's very important for the entire region."

Underneath the fast-moving chart talk sits a slower, more structural argument that got louder as the period progressed. On August 11 one voice argued that "the reserve asset dimension of the dollar is definitely going to decline. I think we could see the dollar down to as little as 40…" — a genuinely extreme endpoint, whatever index it's measured against. The same day produced the counterintuitive companion view that "the dollar is very strong, unusually strong. Our currency should be a lot weaker than it is given our fiscal situation." These two are not really opponents; both are dollar-skeptics, one forecasting reserve-driven collapse and the other complaining the currency is still overvalued given US fiscal math. The July 25 material adds the plumbing behind that unease: "India's demand for dollars has repeatedly exceeded the private supply available at prevailing exchange rates," post-2008 bank constraints have pushed dollar funding into swaps, and regional currency interventions have reliably produced "a temporary rally followed by renewed depreciation." Notably, the corpus never reconciles that offshore dollar scarcity with calls for the index at 40 — the shortage story and the collapse story coexist without a referee.

The tone shift across the window is the clearest pattern of all. Late July is structural and monetary-plumbing in register — funding markets, intervention failure rates, balance sheet constraints. August is faster and more tactical: support levels, moving averages, carry-trade triggers, and even a hint of official-sector involvement in the August 7 fragment "Treasury Takes Control Of Volatility." The constants through both registers are rising yields and a dollar whose problems are framed as cumulative rather than cyclical. What the digest cannot support is any verdict on who has been right — with no names, no links, and a cluster of entries (August 11 and July 28 among them) whose quotes extracted as blank, this page can map the shape of the argument but not score the arguers.
