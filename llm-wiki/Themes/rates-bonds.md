## What the corpus says

If this corpus has a center of gravity in late August 2026, it's the revival of the "debasement trade" — and the ticker counts show it. Rates, bonds and the dollar generate 171 tickered calls and 340 broad views here, dominated by the 10-year yield (^TNX, 66 calls) and the dollar index (DX-Y.NYB, 56), with the yen (JPY=X, 40) as the third leg. The connective tissue across the August 24–27 cluster is a single narrative: the Treasury doubled the long end of its bond buybacks, the dollar sagged to the bottom of its range, and Reuters explicitly tied the move to a softer dollar and renewed debasement fears — with the corollary, as one contributor put it, that when policymakers signal less willingness to let long-term rates clear higher, harder assets naturally get bid.

The most interesting voice is [JD](../People/jd.md), because he embodies the tape's own contradiction. On August 27 he declares himself a dollar bear — "have been for a while" — and marvels that bitcoin is, for the first time in a long time, maybe ever, acting like a real dollar debasement hedge, a move he ties to the Bessent news out of Treasury. Yet the same day, asked about the dollar itself, he sees a bounce setup and admits he's flat. That's structurally bearish, tactically agnostic — an honest posture, and one the Macro Dirt host clearly shares, flagging that the dollar "fell to the bottom of the range at 99 and cannot get up" as important to his thinking. [Daniel Lacalle](../People/daniel-lacalle.md) adds the structural layer from the policy side, arguing the US government is banning central bank digital currencies and forcing a new paradigm for the dollar's role, while another contributor dryly notes that bitcoin treasury companies are struggling because the underlying asset is.

The bond side is more guarded. Anonymous contributors on the 10-year see "some decent short-term shorts to be had in bonds" but aren't interested in pressing, and argue the fiscal impulse isn't strong enough in the back half of the year to produce anything "crazy" in yields. One unnamed host pushes the fiscal-dominance question head-on — what happens when the printer of the currency and the issuer of the bonds announces it will do all these things? — and an August 24 view concedes that long-end yields can fall and the curve flatten, in fact "it probably will happen." Others wonder whether the dollar grinds "begrudgingly" higher in the short term only until policymakers bring out something larger. The through-line is uncomfortable for bondholders: the market no longer fully trusts the long end to clear on its own, and Treasury buybacks aren't fixing that perception.

The yen is the quiet confirmation inside the dollar-bear case. Contributors on August 24–25 put the odds of a Bank of Japan hike at its September meeting — a day or two after the Fed's — at 88–90%, and note that Treasury intervention at the end of July failed to hold the yen up for very long. A weak yen helped fund the dollar system for decades; if the BOJ keeps normalizing while Washington revives debasement trades, that transmission breaks.

The main recorded dissent is refreshingly sober. An unnamed commentator writing in Cantonese argues US dollar hegemony holds for roughly the next decade, getting only gradually diluted as Japan, Korea and China rise in Asia — and that there will be no collapse from 99 to 85 on the index within Trump's remaining two-year term. Weakness as a grind, not a cliff. Given that nearly everything else in this sample leans toward debasement anxiety — and the framing visibly hardens across those four days, from bond shorts to buybacks to bitcoin-as-hedge — that lone counterpoint, thin as its coverage is, deserves to stay in the frame.
