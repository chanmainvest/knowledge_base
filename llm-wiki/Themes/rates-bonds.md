## What the corpus says

The heaviest traffic in this corpus runs through three instruments: 68 calls on the 10-year Treasury yield, 56 on the Dollar Index, and 40 on the yen, out of 173 tickered calls and 362 broad views tagged to the theme. That volume comes with two caveats worth stating up front. First, nearly everything dated here clusters in a single week — August 25 through 28, 2026 — so there is no visible tone shift over time; this is a snapshot of one crowded week, not an arc. Second, attribution is thin: almost every speaker in the extract is unnamed, with [JD](../People/jd.md) the only properly identified voice, which limits how much of a "who's on which side" map this page can honestly draw.

On rates, the center of gravity is stasis. One speaker expects the 10-year to hold in a range around four and a quarter percent, and another assembles the pieces into the view that "interest rates probably are going to stay around where they are now." The hawkish case exists but gets talked down rather than embraced: higher rates "would hurt the housing market... would hurt the economy," but the calamity some people fear is implied to be overdone. The fiscal bears are present — one host raises the nightmare of the currency printer and bond issuer promising "all these things," while another counters that fiscal impulse conditions simply aren't there to drive a crazy move in the back half of the year. Around the edges sit the tacticians and the honest: one concedes there are "decent short-term shorts to be had in bonds" but can't be bothered, and one flatly refuses — "I never make predictions about interest rates" — which is its own kind of signal about how humbling this market has been.

The dollar debate splits by clock speed, not direction. [JD](../People/jd.md) saw it "setting up for a bounce" on August 27 but stayed flat, a hedge that telegraphs low conviction. The same week, another speaker wondered whether the dollar would "begrudgingly go higher still in the short term," and the relative-suck argument got its usual airing — the dollar has held up partly because it's not just the US that's in bad shape. The longest-horizon view comes from the Cantonese-language commentary, which is a nice breadth marker for the corpus: dollar hegemony survives the next decade, diluted at the margins by Japan, Korea and China in Asia, but the dramatic scenario — 99 on the index down to 85 inside Trump's remaining two years — will not happen. Weakness, in that framing, is a structural story for the 2030s, not a trade for this presidential term. That's agreement on the slow erosion and disagreement only about whether anything tradeable happens first.

The yen thread is where policy and market visibly collide: the Bank of Japan might raise rates, but the Treasury intervened once at the end of July and it "didn't hold up the yen for very long" — the market won that round. Worth noting, too, that this theme's bucketing is loose. It has absorbed a Ned Davis buy-thrust signal, Nevada drilling costs of $600–700 a meter, and a pitch for a 120,000–130,000-ounce-a-year mine with a 10-year life — evidence that "rates" in this corpus often means cost of capital for real projects, not just yield-watching. The honest summary of the week: everyone expects the bond market to grind sideways near 4.25%, the dollar bulls lean on relative ugliness rather than American strength, and the live debate is fiscal tail-risk versus fiscal boredom.
