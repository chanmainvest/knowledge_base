## What the corpus says

Broad-market coverage in this corpus is an S&P 500 story first, with everything else a footnote: 13 of the 23 tickered calls sit on the ^GSPC, the Nasdaq (^IXIC) takes four, the dollar index (DX-Y.NYB) appears four times without yielding a single quotable call, and the KOSPI and SPY get one mention apiece. The 82 broader macro views dwarf the tickered ones, which says something about where this debate actually lives — above the index level. The captured window runs from late June to mid-August 2026, and across those eight weeks the recorded mood travels a long arc: concentration anxiety in late June, macro dread through July, and a flush of bullish price targets by mid-August.

The June cluster carries the sharpest index call in the record. On June 23, [JD](../People/jd.md) flagged that "the S&P is now 22% semiconductors, all-time high" and didn't hedge — "I'm very bearish on the index." The same day, [Jeffrey Christian](../People/jeffrey-christian.md) ran a deliberate split between the economy and his own bullish precious-metals stance, arguing the same risks "may be negative for the broader economy but supportive for gold and silver over time," and separately complained that "he wanted the Fed to be less forthcoming with information, less transparent, more secretive" — a jab at Fed secrecy with the target unrecorded. The Hong Kong voices added texture rather than price targets on June 26: [何啟聰](../People/person-001.md) read the KOSPI 200's 10% plunge on an SK Hynix tax scare as proof that leveraged products ("槓桿產品") can turn a single-name wobble into index-wide volatility, while [李聲揚](../People/person-004.md) drew the opposite lesson from the turmoil — that even perfect foresight of how the situation resolves would be "近乎是零幫助，甚至是催命符" to an investor. An unbylined author kept the bear case simpler on June 25: Israel doesn't want the war to stop, and the Middle East is nowhere near real peace.

July belongs to unnamed voices and conditional doom. The logged calls are scenarios rather than forecasts: "let's say S&P drops 10 to 20%" (July 30), the warning that "if they find that the interest rates are going to increase significantly, Nasdaq-oriented stocks get hit" (July 30), and a July 26 S&P claim that "I think that could happen any day" — with the antecedent of "that" unrecorded. The broad views turn almost literary: a July 22 entry describes "a persistent depression-like shortfall," and a July 25 warning sketches a classic policy-error setup — "the danger is that central banks react to the temporary inflationary impact of oil while the underlying economy is already weaken[ing]." The only number on offer all month is a hypothetical 10-to-20 percent drawdown; nobody in the July record commits to a date.

Then the tape flips. By August 10 an unnamed voice wants something to "provide the juice for the S&P to get above 7620," and on August 7 another is "shooting for 8,250 on the S&P 500 by the end of the year and 10,000 by the end of the decade." The same day brings the thesis underneath the targets: a claim that "they just started their intervention tactics like way preemptively when the S&P was off 2 and 1/2%" — official-sector support kicking in at a trivial drawdown. And on August 13 a Chinese-language contributor allows the setup is almost "太看涨" (too bullish) while still expecting a breakout and a rally "像过去几天那样" — like the last few days. From JD's concentration bear in June to five-digit decade targets in August, the recorded sentiment inverted in roughly seven weeks.

The caveat writes itself. The named, linked voices cluster in June; nearly every July–August index call in the digest is unattributed, several August 6 and July 30 entries carry empty quotes, and the dollar index's four appearances produce nothing citable. The bear-to-euphoria arc is genuinely what the captured text shows — but whether it reflects the same people changing their minds or a different, unnamed cohort simply dominating the tape in August is something this record cannot settle.
