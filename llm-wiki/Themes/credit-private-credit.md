## What the corpus says

The private credit file in this corpus is small on tickered calls — five against 78 broad views — but it is unanimous in a way that matters: every one of those calls points down. The sequence starts on June 23, 2026, when Apollo Debt Solutions (ADS), a non-traded BDC, disclosed that repurchase requests had reached roughly 16.8% of outstanding shares. The commentary four days later treats that disclosure as the crack in the dam rather than a one-off.

On June 27, Jeff and Steve build the bear case around it. Jeff's argument on private credit funds is fundamentally a NAV argument — he wants out today because the future net asset value is "a hell of a lot less" than the price a holder can still get — and his read on Apollo is more pointed: the fund has turned cash flow negative, redemption requests keep piling up, and the gate has come down. [Steve](../People/steve.md) widens the lens to the whole sector, naming BlackRock, Blackstone, Blue Owl and Apollo as managers exposed to the same dynamic, and flags that projections for Q3 private credit redemptions were running even higher than current levels. Seven weeks later, on August 12, [Milton Berg](../People/milton-berg.md) makes it thematic: "private credit is really uh collapsing in a way." Within this corpus, the asset class goes from a specific Apollo problem to a systemic call in under two months.

The broad views, though, don't march in lockstep with the calls. The late-August commentary contains the closest thing to a counter-case: Maxy, a host, notes that publicly traded BDCs are trading at discounts to NAV and frames that as a potential entry point rather than a reason to flee, while [Harry](../People/harry.md) speaks from an institutional lens, treating private credit as a replacement for other credit-oriented products — a portfolio building block, not a bubble. The career talk from the same date cuts both ways: direct lending is described as competitive and increasingly crowded with investment bankers migrating in from corporate banking and non-IB roles, which bears would read as late-cycle froth but which also reads as an industry normalizing. An August 24 exchange even wonders aloud what happens "if private credit goes bad" — the mood of analysts stress-testing the scenario rather than living through it.

The macro thread explains why the debate turned hot in August. Views in the corpus describe sticky inflation and a Fed that probably cut a little too early, followed by the concern that rates will "suck up available assets and credit" — precisely the environment in which levered, illiquid credit vehicles get squeezed and redemption pressure becomes gating. An August 27 comment about trade deficits recycling foreign dollars into US assets rounds out the backdrop: the marginal buyer of American credit products is a global dollar glut, and nobody in this file asks what happens when that bid wobbles.

Where does the corpus land? The bears own the tickered tape; the cautious optimists live entirely in the broad commentary, and even they hedge. Conspicuously absent is any voice defending the NAV marks themselves — no one here argues the marks are honest and the redeemers are wrong. That asymmetry is the honest summary of the theme as of late August 2026: the redemption wave is treated as fact, the integrity of private credit valuations as an open question, and the discount-to-NAV trade in public BDCs as the lone, contested synthesis between the two camps.
