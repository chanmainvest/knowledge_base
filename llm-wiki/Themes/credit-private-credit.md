## What the corpus says

The credit conversation in this corpus revolves around one concrete stress point: redemption pressure in non-traded private credit vehicles. The anchor event is the June 23, 2026 disclosure on Apollo Debt Solutions (ADS), where repurchase requests reportedly hit approximately 16.8% of outstanding shares — a level that effectively forces a gate and turns "private credit liquidity" from a talking point into a live problem. Within days, the discussion had generalized from one Apollo vehicle to the whole complex.

The bear camp is coherent and specific. On June 27, [Jeff](../People/jeff.md) laid out the two-part thesis: Apollo's fund had gone "cash flow negative," redemption requests were piling up, and the gate had come down — and behind that, a valuation argument, namely that the future NAV is "a hell of a lot less" than what investors are being marked at, which is why he wanted out (or short) at today's print. [Steve](../People/steve.md), same day, widened the lens to the big managers — BlackRock, Blackstone, Blue Owl, Apollo — noting that projections for Q3 private credit redemptions were running even higher than current levels. By August 12, [Milton Berg](../People/milton-berg.md) delivered the bluntest call in the theme: private credit is "collapsing in a way." The sequencing matters — first a single gated fund, then a sector-wide redemption call, then a flat collapse verdict.

What keeps this from being a one-sided page is the August 24 counterargument. [Harry](../People/harry.md) concedes the mechanics — even well-established funds are seeing redemption rates climb quarter over quarter — but flips the implication: he argues private credit is actually better positioned to "loan to own," restructure distressed situations, and that institutions are using it to replace other credit-oriented products rather than abandon the asset class. The host, Maxy, adds the valuation counterpoint that publicly traded BDCs are trading at discounts to NAV, framing the dislocation as a potential entry rather than an exit signal. That's the real fault line in the corpus: nobody disputes that redemptions are rising and gates are appearing; the split is whether the NAV marks are a slow-motion lie (Jeff's read) or a restructuring opportunity wearing bearish clothing (Harry's).

The ambient chatter in late August shows the debate widening rather than resolving. Unattributed voices on August 24-25 ask plainly what happens "if private credit goes bad," note that direct lending has gotten competitive as investment bankers migrate into the space — an implicit margin-compression argument — and raise the macro wrapper: interest rates that "suck up available assets and credit." The tone has clearly escalated from June's fund-specific incident to something systemic, with the bears now armed with both a liquidity story and a rates story. Worth flagging, though: a large share of the 67 broad views here are unattributed fragments, so the thematic volume is heavier than the attributable substance. The attributable record runs Berg, Jeff, and Steve against Harry and Maxy — collapse versus opportunity — and the corpus, as it stands, doesn't settle it.
