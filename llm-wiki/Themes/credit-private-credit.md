## What the corpus says

The corpus's private-credit coverage is really the story of one summer. On June 23, the narrator's recap flagged the number that seemed to set the tone for everything after: investors had filed repurchase requests on Apollo Debt Solutions (ADS), a non-traded BDC, equal to roughly 16.8% of outstanding shares. Within days, Jeff was out with the harshest read in the corpus — Apollo's fund "now cash flow negative," redemption requests piling up, gates going up — and, more pointedly, arguing that the NAVs investors were being paid out at overstate the true mark: "the future net asset value is a hell of a lot less than what you're going to give me." Steve, the same day, widened the frame from Apollo to the whole manager complex — BlackRock, Blackstone, Blue Owl, Apollo — pointing to projections that Q3 private credit redemptions would run "even higher than they are now." By August 12, [Milton Berg](../People/milton-berg.md) compressed two months of redemption math into a flat verdict: private credit "is really uh collapsing in a way." June was the data; Berg was the obituary.

What keeps this from being a one-sided bear page is that the pessimism about private credit *structures* never quite contaminates the corpus's view of credit as an asset class. On August 28, an unnamed voice notes the shop has had "a more favorable view on credit, so investment grade and high yield, for a few months now" — which, if you take the June redemption panic seriously, is a striking divergence: the listed, transparent end of the credit market stays in favor while the opaque, gated end comes under fire. The real fault line in the debate is NAV trust, and the two camps read the same fact opposite ways. Jeff sees discounts and gates as proof the marks are fiction; Maxy (host), on August 24, sees publicly traded BDCs "trading many of them at discounts to their NAV" and frames it as a buying opportunity. Same discount, opposite conclusions — one camp says the discount isn't big enough, the other says it's a gift.

Harry, also writing August 24, is the least polemical voice here and worth flagging as such: from an institutional lens, private credit is simply replacing "some of the other credit oriented products" — allocation plumbing, not a bubble. That descriptiveness stands in useful contrast to Berg's collapse thesis and Jeff's NAV skepticism, though it does little to resolve them. Around these calls, the broad views add texture rather than direction: sticky inflation and a sense that "we probably were cutting a little too early" (August 24), rates that "suck up available assets and credit" (August 25), and an unattributed observation on August 27 about trade-deficit dollars recycling back into US assets — the plumbing behind all that credit demand.

A caveat on attribution: of the roughly 80 broad views, most are unattributed fragments, so the genuinely named voices — Jeff, Steve, Berg, Harry, Maxy — carry the page, and only Berg and Harry have persistent identities across the corpus. The late-August notes also surface a quieter, sociological bear point worth more attention than it gets: investment bankers are crowding into direct lending, making it "competitive." If the bull case for private credit rests on borrower relationships and selectivity, credential-driven capacity growth is a slow leak nobody in this corpus has yet connected to the June redemptions. The tone arc, in short: June panic, mid-August verdicts, late-August bargain-hunting — with the NAV question left deliberately unresolved.
