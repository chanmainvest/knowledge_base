## What the corpus says

The robotics, automation and autonomy corner of this corpus is thin — two tickered calls and seven broad views — but it stages the argument cleanly, and the fault line is not valuation. It is whether the technology works at all. On the bull's disciplined end, [Chris Semenuk](../People/chris-semenuk.md) went up on Rockwell (ROK) on June 18, 2026, framing the trade around the equipment layer — "companies that are benefiting like Rockwell, Emerson, machine vision" — rather than headline robot makers. His companion view the same day is more guarded on the glamour segment: "the whole humanoid thing is early," though supply-chain companies tell him it's "going to come a lot." Own the picks and shovels, wait on the humanoids.

At the other pole sits [David Woo](../People/david-woo.md), who isn't hedging. His June 15 take on Tesla (TSLA) — logged without a rating, though the language needs none — is that the latest robotaxi trial in Texas "was literally like creating traffic incidents." He doubled down on July 3: humanoid robots, driverless cars — "They're going nowhere. They are absolutely nowh—." Woo is the only categorical dismissal in the set, and his case is operational, not a price call.

The most aggressive conviction arrives last. On August 20, [Andrew Kang](../People/andrew-kang.md) declared the goal of being "one of the largest robotics investors in the world," sized total AI market cap at "at least 10, 20, 30x larger than Bitcoin," and borrowed the MicroStrategy playbook — their TAM was Bitcoin, and at peak they reached around a hundred billion dollars in market cap — while pointing his own TAM somewhere far beyond it. Notably, none of this is a tickered call. The theme's biggest numbers live entirely in the broad-view bucket, which tells you something about how early and how speculative this trade remains even among its truest believers.

The two Chinese-language columnists supply the flanks the bulls and Woo both skip. [梁天卓](../People/person-006.md), writing June 25, is constructive on autonomous driving but argues for the middle path: limited pilot schemes, approval only for systems that pass review, trials confined to designated areas or routes, and mandatory operator disclosure of mileage, takeovers, accidents, near-misses and complaints, with scope expanded or narrowed on the data. [高天佑](../People/person-010.md), two days earlier, files the labor-cost side of the automation debate — mainland China's slowing economy and deteriorating job market, where workers over 35 are treated as middle-aged, less competitive than the young "小鮮肉," and first in line for layoffs. Whether robots create or destroy is a bull-bear question; who absorbs the dislocation is one neither Semenuk nor Kang addresses.

Read chronologically, the tone doesn't converge — it polarizes. June opens contested: Woo's mockery of the Texas robotaxi on the 15th, Semenuk's grounded equipment call on the 18th, the labor and policy essays on the 23rd and 25th. Woo holds his line into July, and by late August the bull case has escalated from machine-vision orders to a 10-30x-on-Bitcoin market-cap claim. With only two tickered calls between them, these pages can't settle anything — but they make the real split legible: Woo says the tech is going nowhere, Semenuk is quietly compounding through the supply chain, Kang is betting it's everything, and the policy and labor writers are documenting the world in between.
