## What the corpus says

Robotics, automation and autonomy is one of the more polarized corners of this corpus: only two tickered calls and seven broad views, but almost no overlap in the camps. Between June and August 2026 the theme collects a picks-and-shovels buyer, a thematic maximalist, a blunt skeptic, a policy pragmatist, and a labor-market worry — five positions, zero fence-sitting.

The bulls split into two very different species. [Chris Semenuk](../People/chris-semenuk.md) is the infrastructure camp: on 2026-06-18 he went up on ROK, naming Rockwell, Emerson and machine-vision suppliers as the real beneficiaries of automation capex. His humanoid view is candidly early-stage — "the whole humanoid thing is early" — but he leans on supply-chain conversations suggesting it arrives sooner than the headline timelines imply. [Andrew Kang](../People/andrew-kang.md), writing on 2026-08-20, is the corpus's maximalist: his stated goal is to be "one of the largest robotics investors in the world," and he frames the opportunity with a MicroStrategy analogy — that fund's TAM was Bitcoin, peaking around a hundred billion in market cap, while he argues total AI market cap will be "at least 10, 20, 30x times larger than Bitcoin." That is TAM logic borrowed from crypto, applied to robots.

[David Woo](../People/david-woo.md) is the counterweight, and he is not subtle. Two days before Semenuk's call, Woo noted Tesla's robotaxi trial in Texas was "literally like creating traffic incidents" (2026-06-15), and by 2026-07-03 he was dismissing the whole complex — humanoids, driverless cars — as "going nowhere." Where Kang extrapolates TAMs, Woo points at the trial footage. The most useful voice between them is [梁天卓](../People/person-010.md) (2026-06-25), who is bullish on autonomous-driving technology but argues for a middle path: approve only systems that pass review, run pilots in designated areas, force operators to disclose mileage, takeovers, accidents, near-misses and complaints, then expand or shrink the footprint on the data. [高天佑](../People/person-015.md) (2026-06-23) supplies the displacement underside of the theme, flagging China's deteriorating job market where workers over 35 are treated as prime layoff candidates — automation-adjacent labor anxiety rather than a robot call, but it belongs to this debate.

The arc over three months is telling: June's "early but coming" framing, July's flat dismissal, August's 10-30x euphoria. Woo and Kang mark the widest spread in the corpus, and Semenuk's supply-chain sourcing collides directly with Woo's trial observations on humanoid timing. Worth noting how thin the ticker coverage is — ROK appears once, TSLA only via a dismissive aside — so this theme is argued almost entirely in prose rather than positioned calls.
