## What the corpus says

The robotics and autonomy thread in this corpus is less a trade dossier than an argument about timing — and the actual ticker-level exposure is thin: just two named calls (Rockwell and Tesla), with everything else pure stance. The cleanest reading of the debate: the bulls are buying automation through the equipment layer, while the loudest skeptic attacks autonomy at its point of execution, and the two positions coexist without ever really engaging each other.

The skeptic fires first. On June 15, [David Woo](../People/david-woo.md) dismissed Tesla's robotaxi trial in Texas as "literally like creating traffic incidents" — no recorded rating, but the tone leaves little room for doubt. Three days later, [Chris Semenuk](../People/chris-semenuk.md) put a buy on Rockwell Automation, framing the winners as "companies that are benefiting like Rockwell, Emerson, machine vision" — equipment and instrumentation, not autonomy software. His companion view the same day is the crux of the bull case: "the whole humanoid thing is early," but supply-chain companies are telling him it arrives sooner than consensus thinks. That's a deliberately hedged way to own the theme — you profit from factories automating whether or not a driverless car ever behaves itself. Woo, unsatisfied, widened his attack on July 3 to the entire category: humanoid robots, driverless cars — "they're going nowhere… absolutely nowhere." The clash here is really about which part of the stack you're pricing: Semenuk's industrials vs. Woo's road-test evidence.

Between the poles sit two Chinese-language contributions that change the question rather than pick a side. [梁天卓](../People/person-006.md), bullish on autonomous driving as a technology, argues for the pragmatic middle path on governance: limited pilots, only systems that pass review, designated areas and routes, and mandatory operator reporting on mileage, takeovers, accidents, near-misses and complaints — with scope expanded or contracted based on the data. It's conditional bullishness: the tech works, the policy hasn't caught up. [高天佑](../People/person-010.md) supplies the darker macro backdrop, bearish on China's labor market — a slowing economy, worsening employment, and workers over 35 already classed as middle-aged and at highest layoff risk against younger competition. Filed under this theme, it's the social ledger the automation bulls rarely price.

Then August brings the speculative pole. [Andrew Kang](../People/andrew-kang.md), on August 20, declares the goal of becoming "one of the largest robotics investors in the world" and reaches for a telling analogy: MicroStrategy's TAM was Bitcoin and it peaked around $100 billion in market cap — his AI TAM, he argues, will make total AI market cap "at least 10, 20, 30x" Bitcoin's. Read the arc across two months and the shift is unmistakable: June's execution skepticism and policy hedging give way, by late summer, to a maximalist TAM framing imported wholesale from the crypto-treasury playbook. With only two tickered calls on the books — one an industrial, one a mockery — this corpus frames the robotics debate far better than it measures it.
