## What the corpus says

The corpus's AI-and-semiconductor coverage is busy but oddly byline-light: twenty-five tickered calls and ninety-one broad views, dominated by SMH and Nvidia at five calls each, yet the most specific price calls in the file carry no name at all. The sharpest disagreement lands on August 10, 2026, when two unattributed SMH calls set targets nearly ninety points apart — one "calling for a move up to 590," the other warning there's "a decent probability we're going to see SMH roll over again and take out the recent low at 503." That phrasing, plus a July 24 note that the strongest stocks "such as Micron Technology (MU)" had escaped their downtrend channel, tells you the tape story: semis corrected into early summer, memory names led them out, and by August the fight is over whether the recovery extends or the index breaks down. Both sides of that fight are anonymous in this digest, which is a real limitation.

The named bulls cluster around memory and private AI infrastructure. [aminvest](../People/aminvest.md) on June 25 argued Micron had practically single-handedly lifted half the market and that many large-cap tech names should keep rising — but paired that with a blunt warning that plenty of AI-related and unrelated companies worldwide will go bust, a barbell view rather than blanket cheerleading. [Mike Alfred](../People/mike-alfred.md), asked about his AI infrastructure holding (Iron) the same week, said he intends "to continue holding" and that it's "unlikely I'd be selling anything." The memory thesis also shows up as a sector call on DRAM/NAND producers, Korean and American alike, suggesting the bulls' conviction runs through the supply chain, not just one ticker.

The named bears are more quotable than precise on price. [Ed Zitron](../People/ed-zitron.md), on June 21, put a clock on the whole thing: asked whether the AI bubble pops in 2026, 2027 or 2028, he answered "I think 2027's the safe bet," and separately gave Nvidia "two, three more earnings calls" before the market stops rewarding guidance that no longer makes it "rock hard" every quarter. [Jeffrey Christian](../People/jeffrey-christian.md) is bearish on the sector for a different reason — his complaint is that AI is "generating a tremendous amount of misinformation, bad information, inaccurate information," a qualitative gripe, not a multiple. [George Noble](../People/george-noble.md) declines to pick a side on the indexes at all: "what the index does, I don't really know. I don't really care. I want to own assets."

Between the camps sits the physical evidence, which cuts both ways. On July 22, unattributed comments noted that even A100 rental rates have gone up and stayed up, and that cloud providers — "the landlords of AI" — have grown comfortable signing long-term contracts. That's genuine demand tightness, and it's the bulls' best exhibit against a bubble-popping timeline; it's also exactly what late-cycle scarcity looks like. By August the macro framing had hardened, with unattributed views flagging high valuations, rising costs and America's growing power and critical-minerals constraints, and one piece framing the capex boom outright as statecraft. The tone shift across the file is real: June's debate was about verdicts — bubble or not — while August's is about levels, constraints and who's paying the electricity bill. Worth flagging for readers: MSFT (two calls), TER and MRVL appear in the tally but carry no quotable detail in this digest, and several quotes arrive truncated mid-sentence.
