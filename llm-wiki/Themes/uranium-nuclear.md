## What the corpus says

The uranium and nuclear file in this corpus is thin but unusually coherent: everything hangs on a single mid-June 2026 discussion featuring MacroVoices host [Erik Townsend](../People/erik-townsend.md), technicians [Patrick Ceresna](../People/patrick-ceresna.md) and [Brent Johnson](../People/brent-johnson.md), and [Chris Semenuk](../People/chris-semenuk.md), captured across digest entries dated June 18 and 19. With just four tickered calls and eight broad views to work from — spanning uranium itself, uranium equities, and the SMR complex — there's no long arc to trace. What the corpus offers instead is a clean snapshot of where this group stood at one moment, and the split is telling: nobody disputes the long-term thesis. The argument is about timing, and about which vehicle you use to express it.

On timing, Townsend is the most specific of the bulls. He calls himself "uber duper bullish long long-term on uranium" ([Erik Townsend](../People/erik-townsend.md)) and puts a date on the trade: "look for late August, early September as maybe when this market starts to catch a bid again" ([Erik Townsend](../People/erik-townsend.md)). Johnson sits in the same camp, and the digest records him using nearly identical uber-bullish language on uranium equities while conceding "there's been some upside retracement in the last few days" — recent gains given back, in other words, which both men read as a feature of an intact bull market rather than the end of one ([Brent Johnson](../People/brent-johnson.md)). Ceresna is the abstention. He isn't bearish, but he won't call a bottom: the uranium charts "remain relatively distributed," and he's "still in the process of trying to discover where these uranium stocks are going to start showing bottoming formations" ([Patrick Ceresna](../People/patrick-ceresna.md)). His stated tell is flows — he's watching for when investors "start to once again flow back into these uranium names" before committing ([Patrick Ceresna](../People/patrick-ceresna.md)). In a panel full of conviction, his position amounts to "show me first."

The sharpest clash in the corpus isn't over uranium at all — it's over the speculative end of nuclear equities. Semenuk is bluntly dismissive of the SMR names: "you don't need to go out and buy" Oklo, Nano Nuclear or NuScale, because "all these are kind of revenue-less, profit-less businesses" ([Chris Semenuk](../People/chris-semenuk.md), [Chris Semenuk](../People/chris-semenuk.md)). His preferred nuclear-adjacent exposure is the boring end of the value chain — regulated utilities, which he frames as 8-to-9% EPS growth stacked on a 3-to-4% dividend yield, roughly 12-to-13% all-in ([Chris Semenuk](../People/chris-semenuk.md)). That's the real fault line here: a commodity bull thesis on uranium shared across the panel, versus a portfolio manager arguing the trade's most-hyped tickers are precisely the worst way to play it.

An honest caveat closes the loop: because coverage clusters on two consecutive days in June 2026, this page cannot chart any shift in tone over time. It captures one room, one week, one argument. Whether the late-August bid Townsend predicted ever materialized, or whether Semenuk's dismissal of the revenue-less SMR cohort aged well, is simply outside what this corpus records.
