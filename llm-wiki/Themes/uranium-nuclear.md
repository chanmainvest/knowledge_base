## What the corpus says

The corpus's uranium and nuclear coverage is small but unusually lopsided toward skepticism. Of the four tickered calls, all three of substance landed on the same June 18, 2026 discussion, and none of them is a buy. [Patrick Ceresna](../People/patrick-ceresna.md) wouldn't commit to anything on uranium equities, saying he was "still in the process" of trying to discover where the stocks start showing a bottoming formation — a chartist's way of saying no bottom has confirmed yet. [Erik Townsend](../People/erik-townsend.md) was the only one willing to put a timing on it, flagging "late August, early September as maybe when this market starts to catch a bid again" — hedged with "maybe," and the word *again* doing real work, implying uranium had been bid before and had since rolled over. The sharpest stance came from [Chris Semenuk](../People/chris-semenuk.md), who extended the caution from the commodity to the hottest corner of the trade, telling listeners "you don't need to go out and buy Oklo or Nano or New Scale. All these are kind of revenue-less, profit-less businesses" — a direct shot at the SMR complex that has been the public face of the AI-datacenter nuclear narrative.

The interesting shape here is what the corpus *doesn't* contain: nobody is arguing the nuclear renaissance thesis is wrong. Ceresna is waiting for confirmation, Townsend is waiting for a season, and Semenuk is objecting to the vehicle, not the fuel. It's a debate about entry points and which instruments actually carry exposure — physical-linked equities versus pre-revenue reactor developers — rather than about whether demand exists. That makes the June cluster a picture of a trade everyone acknowledges but no one in this corpus is willing to front-run.

The August broad views pivot entirely away from trading and toward regulation and disclosure, and they're strikingly unromantic about uranium. In an August 24 Resource Talks conversation with the unnamed CEO of Brazilian Rare Earths, uranium comes up only as something the company deliberately kept *out* of its formal resource estimate — along with niobium, scandium and tantalum — per the host's framing. The stated logic is procedural, not bullish: Brazilian legislation requires uranium to be sold back to the government on a cost-plus arrangement, and until there's "actual definition on what's going to happen in the uranium," management preferred to "just leave it out." In other words, where equity bulls treat uranium as the prize, this operator treats it as a compliance headache that would only complicate a valuation. Meanwhile the August 22 discussion splits on US jurisdictions: one speaker would "be surprised if anything ever gets mined again in New Mexico on a uranium front," while another line in the same conversation calls a state "arguably the most supportive" in the country for uranium mining — the digest doesn't resolve which state that refers to, so it reads either as a genuine split or as a federal-versus-state distinction the fragments don't untangle.

Fair warning about coverage: much of the 33 broad views are truncated fragments from unidentified speakers, so the theme rests heavily on the June 18 calls. But the tone across everything on record is consistent — wait-and-see on the metal, dismissive of the SMR names, and narrowly procedural everywhere else. This is a theme the corpus observes with more caution than conviction.
