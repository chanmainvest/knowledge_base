## The debate

The dollar debate through mid-2026 is less a bull-versus-bear fight than a quarrel over timeframes, and the tape makes that obvious. In mid-June, with the DXY back at 99.50, [Erik Townsend](../People/erik-townsend.md) flagged the level twice in as many days (June 18 and 19) as "testing what was previously resistance as support" — a bearish read on the chart. Two days later [JD](../People/jd.md) went further, calling the dollar's move "a false breakout" and declaring himself "very bearish." The lone loud objection came from [Brent Johnson](../People/brent-johnson.md), who on June 26 pointed to the dollar's run higher alongside falling euro, pound, gold and silver as precisely the setup his long-running milkshake thesis predicts: the index "can fall from time to time," but pullbacks are "setting up the scenario" for the next leg up.

By late August the bear case had matured into two distinct strands. The price-action version came from Massil on August 20: if the recent move is "just the beginning of this trend, then there's a lot of room for further moves against the dollar." The regime-level version came the same day from [Darius Dale](../People/darius-dale.md), who argued the dollar "tends to be debased substantially during Fourth Turnings," particularly against a base form of money — the debasement trade in so many words. Against that, the structural bulls kept their case alive: an unnamed commentator in the same stretch argued dollar hegemony survives the next decade, at most slightly diluted as Japan, Korea and China gain ground in Asia, and that the feared slide — from around 99 down to 85 — simply won't happen inside Trump's remaining two-odd years. Another voice wondered on August 25 whether the dollar "will begrudgingly go higher still in the short term" before some larger market event arrives.

The middle of the distribution is where the consensus actually sits, and it sits there heavily. [Patrick](../People/patrick.md) conceded on August 20 that the move had "neutralized the prior bull market" but balked at calling himself "an outright bear," and the MacroVoices trading desk's framing that day was whether the index just re-enters the 96–100 range that defined 2025 and the first half of 2026. An August 23 take split it explicitly: safe-haven flows support the dollar, fiscal worries undermine it, and the two forces roughly cancel. Worth noting the index has basically honored that view — it sat around 99.5 in June and was still "99" in late August, months of debate with almost no net movement.

The one documented mind-change belongs to [JD](../People/jd.md), and it tracks the corpus itself. From "very bearish" on June 23, he resurfaced on August 27 saying the dollar "looks like it's setting up for a bounce here" — while flat, not long. That drift from conviction bear to neutral bounce-watcher mirrors the overall tally: a nominal "bearish" label resting on three bulls against six bears, with forty-seven neutral entries doing most of the weight-bearing. Strip out the hedging and the real structure emerges — a near-term range trade between 96 and 100 that almost everyone accepts, with the genuine conviction pushed out to the decade question: debasement, per Dale, or persistence, per Johnson and the hegemony camp.
