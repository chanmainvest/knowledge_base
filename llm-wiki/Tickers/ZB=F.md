What's left is a conversation about conditional scenarios — a market everyone is watching and almost nobody is ready to own.

The first real argument on the record came on June 25, aimed at the perennial China-dump scare. One commentator — the digest doesn't preserve the name — argued the feared Chinese liquidation of Treasuries is arithmetic noise: China holds roughly $650 billion of the ~$39 trillion total US debt, a sliver under two percent, so even a one-off dump on the order of $670 billion would have only a limited market impact, with the other top holders — Japan, the UK — able to "help out" absorb it. Call it the structural-composure case: no collapse from foreign selling, however ugly the headlines.

It's worth noticing what that thesis is and isn't: it's not a case that yields are attractive or that inflation has broken the bond's way; it's a risk-off hedge dressed up as a bond call. The bull case for ZB=F in this record only works if something else breaks.

The closest thing to an outright bear argument arrived August 23, when an analyst (again unnamed in the digest) recalled having warned "several months ago" that "the T-bond attempt to hold is failing" when price was around 114 — and noted that the market has since traded on from there, presumably lower, though the quote cuts off before the current level. That's a slow-bleed thesis: support giving way, chart by chart, rather than a macro manifesto about deficits or Fed missteps. It's also left formally unstated as a stance, which keeps the official count neutral.

Read as a timeline, the shape of the debate is: late June dismisses the foreign-selling collapse scenario, mid-August conditions any bond rally on equities cracking, and late August documents technical support failing around the 114 area.
