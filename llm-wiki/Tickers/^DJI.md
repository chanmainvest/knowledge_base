## The debate

The recorded record on the Dow skews distinctly bearish, and it skews analogical. On July 6, an unnamed commentator writing in Cantonese flagged what they see as a bubble of the same magnitude as the early-2000s tech bust and the 2009 Lehman collapse — with the interesting part of the argument being the transmission mechanism rather than the bubble itself: bank stress cascading from the unwind, and a fight over whether the response is aggressive rate cuts or deeper stress in the banking system, either of which whipsaws US markets (July 6, unnamed speaker). Six weeks later, on August 18, a speaker the digest leaves anonymous — though he refers to himself as "Harry," pointing to longtime boom-and-bust forecaster Harry Dent — escalated the bear case to its logical extreme: the Dow would be "lucky" to fall only as much as it did from 1929 to 1932, a decline he puts at 89%. His kicker is the sharpest line in the record: today's Dow, he argues, "is more like the Nasdaq" — meaning the old blue-chip index now carries the concentrated, high-multiple risk profile of the late-90s tech index, so the Depression analogy applies more directly than skeptics assume (August 18, "Harry").

The bull side of this debate is nearly invisible as a live position. The only bullish-flavored entry is the same speaker's own retrospective — people used to tell him "there's no way in the hell the Dow's going to hit 10,000 by 2000" (August 18, "Harry"). That's a credibility claim, not a call, but it's also the clearest evidence of a mind changed over the long arc: whoever this is built his reputation on a boom forecast for the Dow and now anchors the crash case. Whether that track record strengthens or undercuts his 89% call is exactly what the bulls would contest — if any were on the record.

They aren't. The consensus tags three neutral voices, but the digest includes nothing they said, so the middle of this debate — the "overvalued but not 1929" camp — is a blank page. On what's actually documented, the timeline runs one direction: from bubble-and-bank-stress warning in July to full Depression-analogy crash call by August. Take it as a record of the bear argument's escalation, not a verdict on the index.
