## The debate

The most striking thing about the USD/CNY debate in this corpus is how little of it there is: zero bullish calls, zero bearish, eleven entries parked at neutral or watchlist. Nobody is willing to take a side on the dollar-yuan rate, and the argument that has emerged instead is a subtler one about what kind of currency regime China is actually running — managed fixings, negotiation-driven stability, or something more structurally designed. Coverage is genuinely thin: two named analysts and one unidentified commentator carry essentially the whole discussion.

The only concrete numbers on the board come from the unnamed commentator, who on August 26 allowed that there was still "room for mild appreciation" in the renminbi but argued the fastest stretch of appreciation had already passed, with the pair now settling into a balance between 6.6 and 6.98. Notably, that's an evolution from their July 28 stance, when they framed further appreciation as off the table so long as US-China negotiations kept progressing well — stability conditional on diplomacy, essentially — while warning that a widening gap could push capital out of renminbi assets and into US assets. By August 28 they had drifted from forecasting to describing, characterizing the pair as a price simply set fresh each morning by the fixing. Read together, the trajectory is a analyst talking themselves from conditional stability into a specific range and then into mechanic-of-record mode.

Michael Howell offers the structural counterpoint, and it's the closest thing to a bearish argument in the corpus without actually being one. His August 13 framing is that China is running what he calls a "bifurcated exchange rate regime" — maintaining a stable yuan against the dollar externally while devaluing it internally. That's an important reframing of the debate: it suggests the quiet fixing line on the chart overstates the currency's real strength, since domestic purchasing power is being eroded even as the external rate holds. On Howell's read, the stability everyone else is treating as a neutral fact is itself a policy choice with debatable costs.

Lyn Alden rounds out the picture by declining to participate: she keeps the dollar-versus-Chinese-currency pairing on her radar alongside emerging market currencies — she says she watches them often — but as of her June 25 remarks had staked out no explicit call at all. That makes her the pure watchlist entry, and it's telling that even macro commentators who track the pair closely won't commit. The timeline thickens as 2026 progresses — watchlist in June, regime theory by mid-August, a numeric range and fixing commentary by late August — but the directionless consensus never breaks. After a run of appreciation, there's no bull left standing; and against Howell's internal-devaluation thesis, no one has yet stepped up to call the currency cheap. The debate, for now, is entirely about the nature of the peg, not the price.
