## The debate

The Micron debate in this corpus is a clean two-sided split wrapped in a lot of watching: one bullish call, one bearish call, and seven speakers content to observe rather than commit. The two directional calls landed within ten days of each other in June 2026, and they pull in opposite directions — which is why the consensus tag reads "conflict."

The bear case belongs to [David Woo](../People/david-woo.md), who on June 15 compressed the classic memory-cycle argument into a single breath. He conceded that "all the chips that Micron are making right now as we speak are sold out into 2028 for forward sales," before pivoting on the word that matters: "but once capacity co…" The transcript cuts off mid-thought, but the stance is logged as bearish and the shape is familiar — near-term demand is real and contracted, and the entire bear case lives in what happens when new supply arrives. It's a bet on the cycle turning, not a claim that demand is fake.

Five days later, [aminvest](../People/aminvest.md) went the other way, writing in Chinese that Micron is, roughly, one stock capable of dragging half the market higher (June 25). That's the bull framing in one line: not merely a good chip name, but the load-bearing pillar of the whole rally.

The most detailed coverage, though, comes from an unnamed technician whose arc is the closest thing on this page to a change of heart. On July 19 they flagged the damage — Micron had been up over 300% year to date, and "now it's 190. Oh, no." The tone flipped within a week: a July 24 note (again in Chinese) counted MU among "the strongest stocks," having broken out of its descending channel. By August 11 the same voice was reading the consolidation — an inside candle, "volume's drying up," price action compressing — as a setup "for a potential move to $1,000." From drawdown alarm to a four-figure target in three weeks, on candlestick evidence alone.

It's worth saying plainly that the record here is thin. Only two named speakers took a side; no upgrades, downgrades, or price-target revisions from the sell-side appear anywhere in the digest, and the neutral majority's contributions — at least the chartist's — are tape-reading rather than fundamental work. As recorded, the debate is Woo's capacity worry versus aminvest's market-lifting strength, with a technician in the middle who decided the summer dip was a launching pad. Nobody has publicly flipped sides; the July swing from "Oh, no" to breakout confirmation is the only documented shift of tone, and it happened inside a single commentator's own commentary.
