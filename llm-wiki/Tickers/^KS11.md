## The debate

The KOSPI debate is, on paper, barely a debate at all: the corpus tally sits at zero bullish, zero bearish, and five neutral — a "watch" verdict — and nearly every substantive call comes from a single, unnamed cycle-timing analyst (the digest doesn't attach a name to him). What both voices in the record agree on is the backdrop. The guest analyst observed on June 17 that "the KOSPI basically doubled this year," and the cycle analyst recounted on July 5 that Korea had risen two and a half times off its starting point into August — a run that ended when the index "plummeted on July 2nd." What followed is the fact that frames everything since: by August 12 the KOSPI was down nearly 44%, in what the cycle analyst describes as a major crash into a cycle date, panic selling, "margin calls all over the place" — a market that "was liquefied."

The bull case is pure capitulation contrarianism. The cycle analyst's argument is that a crash landing precisely on a cycle date, with forced liquidation everywhere, is itself the signal — exhaustion, not trend — and on that basis he is carrying a long position as of August 12.

The bear case, such as it is, comes from the same mouth on the same day. Despite the long, he expects a retest: the index "gapped into its recovery high date," and in his framework a gap into a recovery high is a warning rather than a launchpad, making him short-term bearish even while positioned for upside. Sharper still is his conditional from that session — drawdowns between 12 and 19% are normal, but anything beyond 19% "suggests something different" and means it's "going to go much lower." Here lies the genuine tension in the record: a 44% collapse clears his own 19% threshold by a wide margin, which by his logic argues the break was structural rather than a routine correction — yet he's bought it anyway. He is long the reversion and braced for the retest, and the digest gives no evidence he has reconciled the two.

As for who changed their mind: nobody cleanly did. The guest analyst's June 17 remark was observation, not a stance, and the July 5 commentary was retrospective framing rather than a call. The only real pivot on record happened inside one analyst's head between early July and August 12 — from narrating a two-and-a-half-times rally to buying the wreckage of it, and even then with a retest caveat bolted on. Notably, no named bear ever put a short on the record; the bearish view exists only as hedging inside a long. The neutral consensus, in that light, looks less like indecision than an honest reflection of a market where the sole analyst with a view is arguing with himself.
