## The debate

The Nasdaq 100 debate in this record is really a story about one technician's whipsaw through August 2026, with a single firm bear — [Milton Berg](../People/milton-berg.md) — planted on the other side. The official tally (1 bullish, 0 bearish, 6 neutral) makes this look like a calm, consensus-bullish tape, but the actual calls tell a jittier story: the most vocal bull spent the month talking himself out of, and then partially back into, the trade.

The unnamed technician (the transcript record doesn't attach a name) started the month constructive. On August 6 he read the index as "overall… still correcting," noting "it's a nine today. Does that stop it? Maybe." — a measured, if cryptic, nod to his count rather than a full-throated buy. By August 12 the tone had turned sharply cautious: "Nasdaq 100, same too. Same situation. You had a panic low, short big rally, a gap, which looks exhausted because it didn't follow through…" That's the pivot in this record — a gap he once might have read as strength he now flagged as exhaustion. Yet two weeks later, on August 25, he flipped back to leaning up, arguing that "if we happen to gap lower it is very possible that we actually get a close above the opening print," while hedging with the expectation of "some explosive volatility in either direction." That last call is the tell: his August 25 stance is less a conviction bull case than a bet on violence in both directions, with a slight upward tilt.

Against that, [Milton Berg](../People/milton-berg.md) delivered the only outright bearish call on the tape, also on August 12 — the same day the unnamed technician turned cautious. Berg's framing was retrospective and confident: the prior action had "proved to be a top in the semiconductor index and the Nasdaq 100 and the Nasdaq." Note that Berg tied the index to the semiconductors, implicitly making the chip complex the swing factor for the whole NDX — a linkage the bull side never directly contests.

What's striking about the shape of this debate is how little actual bull-versus-bear disagreement there is. Nobody in the record argues against Berg's top call; the bulls' strongest August 12 evidence (the exhausted gap) and the bear's evidence (the top) point at the same price action read two ways. And the six neutral stances in the tally essentially decline to fight on either side. The honest summary: one persistent bear, one technician who oscillated from bullish to cautious to conditionally bullish in nineteen days, and a field of abstainers. If the gap-resolve thesis is right, the bulls get the last word; if Berg's top holds, he was early and alone — which is where he usually likes to be.
