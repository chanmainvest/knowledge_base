## The debate

The debate over the Nasdaq right now is lopsided in an odd way: nearly everything in the record is neutral, pattern-matching description, and the directional calls that do exist skew hard bearish. The one explicitly bullish data point isn't even a call — it's David Woo's rueful observation on June 15 that "[s]ince then NASDAQ has added $5 trillion to this market capitalization" as investors chased AI enthusiasm ([David Woo](../People/david-woo.md)) — while his actual position was the opposite: "I'm short. I mean, I've been short for a while, so it's been very painful" ([David Woo](../People/david-woo.md)). That tension — a $5 trillion AI-driven melt-up that keeps punishing the shorts — is the backdrop against which everything else in this digest plays out.

The bear case built steadily through August. On August 6, an unattributed speaker (Bittel or Pal) laid out the technical warning: "every one of the NASDAQ major corrections, the last two all hit weekly nines" — DeMark-style exhaustion counts — and posed the open question of whether the current setup qualifies, with the timing "either six weeks" or so out ([unattributed (Bittel or Pal)](../People/unattributed-bittel-or-pal.md)). On August 12, Milton Berg dropped the most direct call on record: bearish, calling "a top in the semiconductor index and the Nasdaq 100 and the Nasdaq" ([Milton Berg](../People/milton-berg.md)). The same day, another commentator (unattributed in the digest) was assembling historical analogues — the Russell 2000 above its 5-day moving average for 16 straight days while the Nasdaq had previously "declined at least 13% and held its low," and a VIX down eight of nine days with the Nasdaq's 10-day rate-of-change above 10%, a cluster that "took place four" times before. By August 18 that voice had gone full catastrophe: a scenario of "90% on the S&P, 96% on the Nasdaq" for how far stocks fall, and "the NASDAQ down 64%... that could happen by year-end."

Against that, the bull side is thin — essentially momentum and crowding arguments. The same August 6 exchange noted equities holding up despite liquidity concerns, with the Nasdaq "[down] another 70 dips or so" but weathering it ([unattributed (Bittel or Pal)](../People/unattributed-bittel-or-pal.md)). The MacroVoices host flagged on August 13 that Nasdaq positioning "went short another 25,000 contracts," taking specs "net short 35,000 contracts" — a datapoint that cuts both ways, since crowded shorts can fuel squeezes even as they confirm bearish sentiment, and the digest cuts off before the host reveals which way he read it. Notably, none of the August 12–18 statistics are framed as outright bullish calls; they're descriptive setups whose meaning depends on which historical parallel you believe.

As for minds changing: nobody in this record is documented flipping. Woo has simply bled into his short since well before mid-June, and the neutral mass of coverage means most speakers are watching rather than committing. That's the honest shape of the debate — one documented short who's been wrong so far, one unambiguous top call from Berg, escalating crash-scenario talk into late August, and a genuinely unresolved technical question about whether the weekly nine prints. The bears have the microphone; the tape, so far, has been arguing back.
