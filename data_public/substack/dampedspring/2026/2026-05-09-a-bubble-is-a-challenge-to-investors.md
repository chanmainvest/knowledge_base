# A bubble is a challenge to investors.

- Publication: dampedspring (dampedspring)
- URL: https://dampedspring101.substack.com/p/a-bubble-is-a-challenge-to-investors
- Published: 2026-05-09
- Audience: everyone

I think the equity market may have shifted to a bubble regime. If so, what does it mean for investors?

[![The Big Short GIFs | Tenor](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ygpr!,w_1456,c_limit,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F19bbdf65-fa7c-4a1d-8d88-af5ee8ce8aa1_251x148.jpeg "The Big Short GIFs | Tenor")](https://substackcdn.com/image/fetch/$s_!Ygpr!,f_auto,q_auto:good,fl_progressive:steep/https%3A%2F%2Fsubstack-post-media.s3.amazonaws.com%2Fpublic%2Fimages%2F19bbdf65-fa7c-4a1d-8d88-af5ee8ce8aa1_251x148.jpeg)

Based on everything I do here at dampedspring.com to understand markets I believe the equity markets have entered into a bubble phase. While this may seem like a provocative statement and you may think I am calling the top of the stock market that can’t be farther from the truth. Bubbles are impossible to define ahead of time and just as impossible to predict their path to resolution. What appears to be a bubble can resolve over time. What is a bubble can take years to pop. One thing about bubbles however is in retrospect everyone agrees that the bubble existed and should have been obvious.

My career started just as an equity bubble popped in 1987. By the late nineties the Internet bubble was blown and popped. By 2006 the housing bubble began inflating only to pop in 2008. The result of the GFC slowly inflated a bubble in government bonds that persisted in most countries for a decade and finally had its blowoff top in 2021 and popped. Today, massive investment in AI is flowing through to equity markets which is blowing a bubble. When it will pop is uncertain.

Very few predicted the 1987 Crash ahead of time but some did. The internet bubble was talked about through its entire 4 years of inflating. The GFC bubble was predicted by many but missed by most. The bond bubble was called by many way too early and often and was extremely persistent and lasted years.

Given, the fame and fortune that can come from correctly “calling a bubble” financial market participants are constantly calling out bubbles that don’t exist. Even serious people who are sharing their well researched views for purely altruistic reasons or actually putting their money where their mouth is and positioning themselves for a bubble top are often wrong and even more often early. Nonetheless, I believe the global equity market has entered a bubble phase. I may be right or wrong and I am likely to be early but that is what I see and I have my reasons. My reasons for calling out this bubble should be mostly irrelevant to you.

Why does it matter to understand whether financial markets are in a bubble regime if you can’t tell how long it will last? This article will try to explain.

The reason for calling out this market as a bubble is because trading and investing during a bubble regime, based on my work and experience, is a fundamentally different process for the active investor, more so by orders of magnitude for the leveraged investor, and even modestly for the passive investor.

As you probably know I am a passionate advocate for almost all investors to stop trying to time markets and simply own a low cost balanced portfolio of bonds, tips, stocks, commodities and gold constructed to match your personal risk target. I think timing markets is extremely difficult and most of us can’t do it. Given that view i’m going to focus this article on the challenges most of us will face in a bubble regime. They are unique to bubble regimes

*Note: Im starting with passive you should read this first. But if your a degen market timer scroll to the last section*

**How to invest as an unleveraged relatively passive beta investor in a bubble regime.**

The big moving parts for such an investor are

1. Risk target, cash, and leverage

2. Allocations

3, Rebalances

4. Timing of converting savings accrued from work to beta

*Risk target, cash, and leverage*

A bubble regime is often one of fairly low realized volatility with variable but lowish implied volatility. What makes bubbles different than normal regimes is extreme trending behavior AND the sharpness of its reversal and new trend. Ahead of the bubble popping the combination of lowish vol and trending lulls investors into leveraging up their portfolio. Including not rebalancing into rallies.

In 2020/21 bond buyers saw no risk in buying 75bp yielding 10 year UST. They didn’t move much and had positive carry. Banks leveraged up like crazy INTO the bubble (SVB obviously but even BofA). Risk models demanded leveraging up.

The number one thing NOT to do in a bubble regime is to use traditional measures of forward looking risk. Not only is risk underestimated in a bubble regime it’s also much much more tail-y in both directions.

*Note: as you read this article some of you who are options savvy may think that using options is a good way to address some of these regime related issues. I think not unless you are extremely sophisticated but will cover at some later time.*

Assuming you have a risk target it's perfectly fine to maintain your risk target throughout a bubble regime. In fact I strongly advocate it as you have no idea when the bubble will pop or if it exists at all. BUT bubble regimes tend to have lower volatility and excellent portfolio diversification benefits Which will show up as positively skewed p/l will low drawdown and little pain. The natural reaction when you have what appears to be little risk is to add risk. In fact that natural reaction is part of the mechanics for the inflating of the bubble.

So what’s the practical concrete thing I do? Stepping back, in normal regimes I need to have a forward looking estimate of my portfolio volatility. That estimate fluctuates. When the forward risk is low I leverage up and when risk is high I deleverage. BUT I have learned that can cause significant desired leverage in very calm markets which is super dangerous. To deal with that I have a max long position for more my portfolio. What that does is prevents me from leveraging up in extremely low volatility environments. Which brings me to bubble regimes In bubble regimes I simply lower my max risk threshold. In other words my normal setting for my portfolio size is low risk at 50% cash and high risk at 130% assets (30% leverage) in bubble regimes I lower that to 110% assets. No matter how low risk my portfolio appears I will not leverage it much at all.

*Allocations*

A passive portfolio built to live through all regimes should have a balance of progrowth, anti growth, pro inflation and anti inflation assets. Bubble regimes obviously favor the asset that is most exposed to the bubble drivers but bubble regimes still call for balance. My desired asset allocation doesn’t change in bubble regimes. However, bubbles create tremendous fomo and frustration from owning diversifying assets. Sticking to your balance will allow you to own plenty of the bubble asset. While the concrete thing I do is NOTHING it’s resisting the urge to fomo that is the challenge.

*Rebalances*

If you have a desired asset allocation, market moves in all environments cause your portfolio to become unbalanced and more risky due to its lack of balance. The way investors manage that problem is by rebalancing the portfolio to the desire weight based on some rules. Those rules are tradeoff between costs and risk. Every rebalance trade has a cost. Also if you are rebalancing often the rebalancing itself may churn your portfolio. But without rebalancing you end up holding more risk. While I don't want to hold as much risk in bubble regimes generally I also know that bubble regimes have stronger trends. My adjustment to my passive portfolio is to decrease rebalancing and accept higher risk because the trending nature of the regime decides the cost of rebalancing.

*Timing of converting savings accrued from work to beta*

Let me frame this idea first

The bottom line is one should always be invested in beta. But when you turn your hard earned cash whether from your salary or lump sum is about how “you” should decide how to invest a lump sum. It’s not me saying how. It’s what you need to consider about yourself to make the move. I don’t know you. You may not know you. Get help from people who know you.

Thread on how to invest new money 101.

I have had this discussion with a few of you in DM’s. The question is whether to invest all at once or spread out over time. Some people call that dollar cost averaging (DCA). I will tell you the economic trade offs you are making, but..

It comes down to your own personal emotional responses to each choice.

Firstly the economics.

If you are investing the money for long term beta every single day that you are in cash has a negative expected value. Owning assets at virtually any price has a positive expected value. If it had a negative expected value prices wouldn’t be sustainable as everyone with assets would sell and raise cash. Because why take risk for no expected return.

Of course some days are the worst possible days to buy assets and others are the best possible days. Unfortunately unless you have “Alpha” there is no way to know what day it is. We will come back to that point in a moment. So each morning asset prices are fair and pay a positive risk premium over cash. That strongly favors investing the whole lump sum at once. That’s pretty much the economics. If you have no alpha and you are trying to invest for the long term lump sum is the way to go. Take what you earn or any other lump sum and mechanically invest.

How about when you are trading for alpha returns. In this case missing out on alpha by spreading out your execution over time is extremely costly. If you have alpha act immediately in the size that reflects your edge.

Now let’s get back to reality. You probably don’t have alpha. So you probably are considering a long term beta investment. As mentioned above full lump sum is the better economic approach. In addition it almost always works over the long term. BUT. BUT. Psychology is an issue

You have to understand how you will react to the choice of Lump sum or spreading out going against you and if that emotional pain will cause you to do something rash. What do I mean? Let’s say you run out tomorrow and invest your whole lump some because Andy said it was the economic best idea. And wham you get rug pulled. If the emotional pain then causes you to puke some or all of what you bought you are almost certain to then pay higher to get reinvested. However if you get rug pulled and don’t give a crap because you are in it for the long term you are well suited to investing mechanically.

That is a big difference.

Similarly if you decide to spread out your buying and over 12 days at the same amount. And after you buy 1/12 the first day and the market rips higher you beat yourself up for stupidly not listening to Andy and then rashly abandon your plan and put the other 11/1 In the market 5% higher on Fomo I guarantee you are going to puke some or all a few days or weeks later. If instead your chill and say no big deal I have a plan and I’ll put money in a month from now and stick to my plan. You’re all good. The reason you chose to spread out was good when you first decided because you self evaluated and knew that rug pull on lump sum was going to feel worse and cause rash behavior.

The point is you need to know whichever choice you make it requires you visualize the negative outcome of that choice and the pain it will cause you upfront. Be self aware. Make an informed choice of which possible pain you would prefer to feel and then stick to the plan you chose.

Which pain will cause rash behavior more. lump sum then short term rug pull or spread out buying and fomo pain causing you to chase?

So with that background that choosing the timing of your investing new cash is really about your sensitivity to fomo or feeling dumb for buying high AND the economics say buy immediately when you get new cash the bubble regime strategy “should” remain the same. However and this is the big deal. A bubble regime puts your emotions into overdrive. FOMO is way worse. Rug pulls are way more painful. It’s time to re-examine who you are not just generally but in this turbocharged environment.

In summary, for the passive balanced investor who manages their portfolio to a desired risk target, has a balanced allocation, rebalances, and is constantly investing wages and earnings tweaking ones plan during bubble regimes is perhaps not a big shift but it is a concious decision. Its enemy is FOMO which is extremely strong during a bubble. It deals with that enemy by allowing some leverage but less than normal, it stands strong on balanced allocation but also allows rebalances to be to let the trend run a bit and perhaps most important it requires a rethink of who you are and what will really cause you the most trouble. If you are fomo oriented it will push you to invest mechanically every time you get. New lump sum and if you are more pained from buying highs it will set you up to buy dips well.

**Bubble regime implications for active and leveraged investors**

Though it doesn’t define bubble, bubbles always have some sort of very strong trending markets with often parabolic episodes. While every day, in normal times, some idiosyncratic asset is going parabolic the nature of bubbles is broad parabolic moves in very large asset classes or asset class sectors. Trading parabolic moves in major assets classes and sectors is the challenge

The big difference in “alpha” strategies in Bubble regimes vs normal regimes are

1. The goodness of alpha likely falls. What works in normal markets doesn’t work as well. Size according to lower expected edge.
2. Momentum strategies become much more valuable while mean reverting strategies fall apart use trailing stops but not martingale in case the stop is the other side. Stay out of the way on the fading side. No reason to be a hero.
3. While bubbles are inflating, perceived risk drops, which encourages levering up strategies at exactly the time when they should be left alone or even deleveraged.
4. Relative value relationships can diverge dramatically size these with that expectation. Bubble divergences are not in your sample set .
5. Non bubble assets (all the stuff no one cares about) goes dead for alpha collection pushing participant to seize small alpha opportunities in above normal size to chase any performance available. Don’t do this.
6. The bubble itself impacts the economy and policymakers, increasing the unpredictability of policymaker actions trading policymaker action is tougher. They don’t understand what’s happening any better than you do.
7. Perhaps the most devastating mistake made in bubble regimes is fading the move and worse if it’s done repeatedly in a semi martingale way. Shorting on the way up repeatedly is a common mistake. But buying after the bubble pops repeatedly has been the path to ruin in almost all bubble episodes I have studied. The exception is the complete and sudden full collapse of 1987.
8. Contagion - In both the way up and the way down a bubble regime is prone to major investor forced liquidations on the way up it tends to be isolated to the bubble asset pushing short to liquidate but if you paid attention to the vol market in in 1997 -2000 you know that the composition change from a massive move in tech caused a complete reset to the vol regime and LTCM and others were killed in part by the equity rally and they didn’t think they were even short equities. Forced liquidations also generate contagion. While most bubble inflations have forced short seller liquidations it’s much less common for that to have a contagion. But it can’t be ignored particularly given 5 above. Post bubble popping every bubble has been contagious. Correlations of risky assets goes up as safe havens get sold to pay bubble asset margin calls, leverage providers and end investors pull money from anyone afloat and of course in the GFC the leverage providers themselves liquidate. the message on this particular section is the other side of a bubble always goes the same way. That means all active leveraged strategies need to size themselves to be able to exit before the top because after the top it’s too late
9. The popping of a bubble has always resulted in Main Street pain. The real economy after a bubble pops is going to be impacted. In a bubble regime while it's happening before it pops investors need to fully game the post bubble path. Unfortunately setting up for that path during the inflating portion won't work. The pain has to happen. But it doesn't have to happen to you.

Anyway, these are the big things. The breadth and depth of alpha extraction strategies is far too broad for me to provide concrete steps to take. My goal for this section is to remind the reader of how this is going to go. If we are in a bubble it will pop. If you believe me and we are in a bubble now start preparing your plan of action as it inflated and when it pops. If you don't believe me I still suggest the effort but you can certainly ignore me and as my old boss said “Keep dancing”
