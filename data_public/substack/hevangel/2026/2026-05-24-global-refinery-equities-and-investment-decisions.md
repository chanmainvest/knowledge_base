## Market Backdrop and Recent Cycle History

Refining has moved through **three very different mini-cycles** since 2021. First came the **post-Covid demand recovery**, which tightened utilization and restored pricing power. Then came the **2022 Russia/Ukraine product-dislocation phase**, when diesel and middle-distillate cracks exploded and refiners generated windfall cash flows. The market then **normalized in 2023**, but margins stayed above pre-2020 norms because inventories remained tight and product logistics were still imperfect. In **2024**, margins compressed as new capacity entered from the Middle East, Africa, and Asia, while Europe and parts of Asia showed weaker demand and biofuel economics remained uneven. In **2025**, the cycle improved again, especially for operators that executed well operationally: Marathon posted **94% full-year refining utilization** and **105% margin capture**, while PBF moved from a **2024 full-year operating loss** to a much better 2025 result even before Martinez fully normalized. In **2026**, the Iran war and closure/disruption around Hormuz created another super-spike in crude and product differentials, but peace-talk headlines have already started to unwind part of the crude premium.

**Refining cycle since 2021**

* **2021** — Demand recovery and utilization rebound
* **2022** — Russia-Ukraine shock and record diesel/product cracks
* **2023** — Margins normalize but stay above pre-Covid levels
* **2024** — New refinery capacity and softer non-US demand compress margins
* **2025** — Operational recovery and better capture for strong operators
* **2026** — Iran war and Hormuz disruption spike crude and products, then partial unwind on peace hopes

The demand picture matters as much as crude. The IEA’s April 2026 Oil Market Report cut its 2026 oil-demand view to a **year-on-year decline of 80 kb/d**, versus a prior expectation of growth, and projected a **1.5 mb/d contraction in 2Q26**, its sharpest quarterly decline since the Covid shock. That matters because refinery equities do **not** simply track oil up or down; they track the spread between refined products and feedstocks, plus the survivability of demand. In other words, a moderate, logistics-led oil shock is often bullish for refiners, but a prolonged scarcity shock that destroys end demand is eventually bearish.

U.S. operating conditions remain fundamentally constructive. The EIA’s weekly refinery utilization readings around late April 2026 were still close to **90%**, which is high enough to keep the product market tight when there is a disruption in seaborne crude and products. The same EIA chokepoint work shows that the Strait of Hormuz handles roughly **one-fifth of global petroleum liquids trade**, making the current conflict uniquely important to diesel, jet, and shipping spreads.

The stock-market implication is straightforward. **Refinery equities do best when crude is tight enough to widen cracks, but not so tight that throughput collapses or a recession crushes mobility and industrial demand.** That is why the best entry points in this industry often occur **after the first crude panic**, when the market starts to distinguish between feedstock winners and demand losers. That is the phase we are entering now. This is an inference from the historical cycle and the current 2026 price retracement.

## Scenario Analysis

The current market is defined by one question: **does the 2026 Iran war continue to tighten physical markets, or does the crude premium collapse faster than product cracks?** The IEA’s April 2026 base case already assumed severe disruption; the EIA’s April 2026 conflict-case work showed Brent near **$115 in 2Q26** and **$106 in 2H26** if shut-ins abate, but as high as **$135 in 2Q26** and **$146 in 2H26** if Strait disruptions persist through year-end. Meanwhile, the market has recently moved from extreme panic to conditional optimism, with Brent dropping below $100 on May 6–7 as U.S.-Iran deal headlines surfaced.

**Current state: war premium easing but not gone**

* **No escalation / ceasefire**

  + Brent 85–95
  + Cracks normalize
  + Refiner stocks retrace but stay above 2024 lows
* **Limited strikes / periodic disruption**

  + Brent 100–115
  + Diesel and jet cracks stay elevated
  + Best setup for MPC and VLO
* **Regional war**

  + Brent 120–140
  + Initial crack spike, then demand destruction risk
  + High-beta names outperform early, then diverge
* **Full blockade / sustained Hormuz closure**

  + Brent 140–180+
  + Throughput and recession risk dominate
  + Short-term stock spikes, then much wider dispersion

## Supply Chain and Structural Drivers

The big-picture refinery call still starts with **feedstocks and chokepoints**. The Strait of Hormuz remains one of the world’s most important energy chokepoints, moving around **20 million barrels per day** of petroleum liquids. The IEA has already described the current disruption as the largest supply shock in oil-market history. That matters because refiners with exposure to **alternative crude slates, Atlantic Basin sourcing, or domestic U.S. barrels** can keep running and sell into exceptionally profitable product markets while weaker systems scramble for feedstock.

That is why **crude flexibility** matters more than usual. Valero’s Gulf Coast system has been explicitly benefiting from renewed access to **Venezuelan heavy crude flows**, which management and local reporting tied to stronger margins in Q1 2026. Neste made the opposite but equally important point: it said its production assets are far from the conflict area, it sources crude mostly from the **North Sea**, its renewable-feedstock chain is diversified, and its feedstocks are **not transported through Hormuz**. The same war does not affect every refiner in the same way.

Utilization and logistics are still constructive for the sector. The EIA’s weekly data showed U.S. refinery utilization close to **90%** in late April 2026. OMV reported that its European refining indicator margin more than doubled in Q1 2026 to **$13.9/bbl**, while Neste’s Oil Products margin reached **$23.0/bbl**. Those are exactly the sorts of signals one wants to see when owning refinery equities: refineries that are actually running, distillate tightness, and operators able to capture the dislocation.

Regulation is the other half of the story. In the United States, biofuel policy remains important. HF Sinclair said small-refinery RIN waivers added **$313M** to adjusted refinery gross margins in Q4 2025. Neste highlighted the constructive impact of the finalized U.S. Renewable Fuel Standards on its U.S. business. In Europe, OMV and Neste remain exposed to transition economics, carbon-heavy compliance structures, and capital-allocation pressure between legacy refining and lower-carbon fuels. And politically, the war has already revived discussion of **windfall taxes** on energy profits in Europe and the U.K.

The practical implication is that the sector should not be treated as a single macro trade. The market is now separating into **feedstock winners**, **logistics winners**, **policy beneficiaries**, and **balance-sheet weak links**. That separation is why my top picks are not the highest-beta names, but the names that can survive multiple paths. This is an inference from the company disclosures and current freight/war setup.

## What the Market May Still Be Missing

The market still tends to reduce refinery investing to a one-line view on oil prices. That is too crude. The stronger second-order drivers right now are **diesel and jet cracks**, **shipping and insurance premia**, **feedstock substitution**, **inventory accounting**, and **policy asymmetry**. Phillips 66’s Q1 2026 was a useful reminder: the quarter was hit by **$839M** of derivative mark-to-market losses even though the physical value of its inventory was rising. Reported earnings can therefore understate or overstate real economics in any given quarter.

Another underappreciated issue is that **carbon and biofuel policy can swing cash conversion by hundreds of millions of dollars**. HF Sinclair’s 2025 waivers are a concrete example. Neste’s U.S. renewable-fuel commentary is another. In a world where investors are focused on Hormuz headlines every day, these “boring” policy details can move realized cash flow almost as much as a several-dollar move in crack spreads.

There is also a structural misunderstanding around **small-cap refinery beta**. Many investors assume smaller refiners are simply levered versions of large refiners. They are not. They usually carry **more operational concentration, more regional policy risk, less balance-sheet room, and more quarter-to-quarter noise from outages or derivatives**. That is precisely why I prefer **MPC/VLO** as core positions and relegate **PBF** to a tactical bucket. This is an inference from the balance-sheet and earnings-release evidence already discussed.

### Open Questions and Limitations

This report is strongest on the **U.S. listed refiners** and on **current macro/supply-chain dynamics**. The global comparison is directionally useful, but it is not perfectly apples-to-apples because **OMV**, **Neste**, and especially **Reliance** have broader business mixes than the U.S. pure plays. Some ratio work is therefore necessarily **normalized or approximate rather than exact**, especially for EV/EBITDA and FCF yield outside the cleanest U.S. comparables. Reliance is discussed as the key Asian benchmark, but I did not treat it as a pure-refining valuation comp because that would materially overstate the precision of the comparison.
