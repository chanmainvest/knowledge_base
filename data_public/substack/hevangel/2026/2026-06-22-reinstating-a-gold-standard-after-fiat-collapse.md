## Executive Summary

A return to a gold standard after a hypothetical collapse of today’s fiat money system is **conceptually possible** but, in most plausible real-world cases, **not the most likely durable outcome**. The historical record shows that gold-based systems could deliver long-run nominal anchoring and fixed exchange-rate stability, but only under conditions that are far less available today: relatively flexible wages and prices, smaller welfare states and banking systems, lower political tolerance for recession and deflation, and strong international coordination. Modern research also indicates that a gold standard cannot avoid short-run price and output instability when gold flows or external shocks force monetary contraction; and in crisis conditions, defending convertibility can intensify bank runs and depressions rather than stop them.

Technically, the biggest obstacle is **reserve adequacy relative to modern money and credit**. Official gold stocks are large in absolute terms, but small relative to contemporary monetary liabilities. World Gold Council data show that central banks account for roughly one-fifth of all gold ever mined, while the ECB notes that gold became the second-largest global reserve asset at market prices in 2024 because prices rose and central banks kept buying. That is evidence that gold remains important as a reserve asset; it is **not** evidence that it could smoothly back modern monetary systems at old-style par convertibility without either a very large gold-price reset, a drastic shrinkage of money and bank balance sheets, or both.

A concrete illustration makes the scale problem visible. Using U.S. data only as an example, March 2026 monetary base was about **$5.46 trillion**, and official U.S. gold stock was about **261.5 million fine troy ounces**. A full 100% base-money cover would therefore imply a gold parity of roughly **$20,900 per ounce**, before even attempting to cover broader bank money. That makes clear why any modern re-adoption would almost certainly require either a **massive gold revaluation**, a **narrow base-money-only convertibility regime**, or a **partial/managed backing system** rather than a classic gold standard.

The most plausible post-collapse replacements are therefore **hybrid, not purely metallic**: a re-founded **rules-based fiat regime** with a stronger nominal anchor; in some countries, a temporary **hard external anchor** such as a currency board or foreign-currency adoption; and, increasingly, a sovereign monetary core supported by **CBDC or tokenized reserve infrastructure** and tighter bank-liability design. Gold is more likely to re-emerge as a **reserve, collateral, and confidence asset** than as the dominant everyday unit of account.

Private digital alternatives have narrower but real roles. **Decentralized cryptocurrencies** remain poor candidates for core national money because official institutions continue to find that they do not reliably perform the standard functions of money and are primarily speculative. **Stablecoins** can improve payments, especially cross-border payments, but official analyses stress that they are run-prone and, at systemic scale, need very strong backing and regulation to preserve par convertibility and the singleness of money. **Narrow banking** or full-reserve transaction money can reduce run risk materially, but it changes how credit is funded and reduces monetary elasticity.

The most likely timetable is not an overnight switch to a clean new standard. In the best cases, stabilization would take **months**, with institutional redesign over **years**. In worse cases, there would be an extended period of **bank holidays, multiple monies, capital controls, contract redenomination disputes, foreign-currency substitution, and parallel markets**, with gold and foreign cash functioning informally before a formal regime is rebuilt.

## Assumptions and Framework

Several critical parameters in the question are **unspecified**, and they matter enormously to the answer. The most important unknowns are: the **speed of the fiat collapse**; whether any state still has the political capacity to tax, borrow, and enforce legal tender; whether payment infrastructure remains operational; the distribution of credible reserve assets across countries; and the degree of domestic and international political willingness to accept recession, capital controls, or outright contract rewriting. Because those parameters are unknown, this report evaluates multiple regimes against the same criteria rather than assuming a single collapse path.

The analytical framework used here compares candidate monetary systems across six dimensions the user requested: **price stability, monetary policy flexibility, financial stability, scalability, governance, and transition feasibility**. This is the right lens because crisis literature shows that post-collapse regimes fail less often from abstract theoretical flaws than from three practical mismatches: insufficient reserves, inability to act as lender of last resort, and inability to build durable political legitimacy. That is true under gold, exchange-rate anchors, dollarization, stablecoin systems, and even CBDC-based sovereign money.

A final important framing point is that some items on the alternative list are **monetary standards**, while others are better understood as **institutional overlays or payment architectures**. A gold standard, currency board, or inflation target is a nominal anchor. A CBDC is not, by itself, a nominal anchor; it is a **form of sovereign money and payment infrastructure**. Likewise, narrow banking is a balance-sheet architecture, not a full macro-monetary regime on its own. That distinction matters because some proposals improve settlement, transparency, or run resistance without solving the deeper question of what ultimately anchors the unit of account.

## Historical Evidence on Gold Standards

### What the classical gold standard did well

The pre-1914 gold standard worked better than many later variants because it functioned as a **credible rule**, and because economies were more willing—or forced—to adjust through wages, prices, output, and migration. IMF research finds that the apparent smoothness of the classical system reflected relatively rapid adjustment to shocks rather than an absence of shocks. Bordo and Kydland also argue that the gold standard operated as a **contingent rule**: governments could suspend convertibility in extraordinary circumstances, but credibility depended on markets believing that parity would later be restored. That combination delivered long-run confidence and exchange-rate predictability, even if it imposed painful domestic adjustment.

Modern theoretical work partially vindicates the historical intuition behind gold’s appeal. Recent Philadelphia Fed work finds that the gold standard can generate **long-run price-level stability**, in the sense that the price level converges over time, and not explode indefinitely as under a badly managed fiat regime. Defenders of gold therefore still have a serious point: tying money to a scarce external asset can constrain discretionary overissuance, and that can be valuable in low-trust states or after major inflationary breakdowns.

### Why interwar gold became destructive

The interwar gold standard is the most relevant cautionary case for a post-collapse restoration because it combined financial fragility, political stress, and incomplete credibility. Bernanke and James summarize the core problem clearly: a mismanaged interwar gold standard transmitted monetary contraction and deflation across countries, and the constraints it imposed on central banks made banking panics more damaging. Eichengreen and Temin likewise argue that gold-standard commitments helped draw the world deeper into the Great Depression, while Eichengreen and Sachs show that countries leaving gold earlier generally recovered faster.

The interwar system also revealed a structural asymmetry that remains highly relevant today: **deficit countries were forced to adjust faster than surplus countries**. Bernanke and James describe this asymmetry as a major flaw, because it meant that countries losing gold had to contract, while countries gaining gold were under much less pressure to offset the contractionary effect by expanding. In a modern context—with much larger debt stocks, more leveraged banks, and more politically salient unemployment—that asymmetry would likely be even harder to sustain.

### Bretton Woods shows the international constraint

Bretton Woods was not a classical gold standard but a **gold-dollar system**, and its history matters because it was the last large-scale attempt to preserve convertibility within a modern welfare and financial state. Federal Reserve history notes that the system initially looked secure partly because the United States held about three-quarters of the world’s official gold reserves after World War II. But once global demand for dollar liquidity grew, confidence in dollar convertibility came under strain, and Nixon ended convertibility in August 1971 amid a looming gold run and rising inflation. IMF historical analysis likewise argues that the collapse of Bretton Woods prompted a rethinking of the idea that fixed parities automatically produce stability; in practice, they can generate rigidity and delayed adjustment instead.

The lesson is not simply that “gold failed.” It is that **gold-linked systems fail when the reserve asset cannot grow, or be redistributed, in line with global liquidity needs without producing confidence crises**. That logic would be even more difficult to manage in a world with huge cross-border balance sheets, offshore dollar liabilities, electronically accelerated bank runs, and polarized geopolitics.

### Historical record: regime-by-regime summary

**Classical gold standard**

* Main strengths: Long-run anchor, exchange-rate predictability, fiscal discipline
* Main constraints: Required flexible prices/wages and confidence in eventual restoration after suspensions
* Core lesson for a post-fiat world: Gold can anchor expectations, but only with strong political tolerance for adjustment

**Interwar gold standard**

* Main strengths: Nominal continuity after war
* Main constraints: Deflation bias, banking fragility, asymmetric adjustment, poor cooperation
* Core lesson for a post-fiat world: Restoring gold into a fragile financial system can deepen depression

**Bretton Woods gold-dollar system**

* Main strengths: Stable parities for a period, postwar reconstruction support
* Main constraints: Reserve-currency asymmetry, gold-run risk, inadequate adjustment mechanisms
* Core lesson for a post-fiat world: Gold-linked systems break when liquidity needs outgrow the credible reserve base

## Reinstating Gold After Fiat Collapse

### The modern case for and against gold

The strongest modern defense of gold is not that it is magically stable in all conditions; it is that it acts as a **credible external commitment device**. Bordo and Kydland frame the gold standard as a rule that constrains surprise inflation and policy opportunism, while recent gold-standard modeling confirms that gold can produce long-run price-level reversion. For countries emerging from hyperinflation or extreme institutional breakdown, that kind of externally visible constraint could have political value.

The strongest modern critique is that the same constraint is exactly what makes gold dangerous in crisis. Philadelphia Fed work finds that the gold standard cannot prevent short-run price movements driven by external disturbances, and that policymakers have fewer options than under fiat. Fernández-Villaverde and Sanches also show that gold scarcity can produce suboptimal liquidity and expose domestic economies to foreign shocks; further, when private inside money grows on top of a gold base, banking crises can transmit internationally through specie flows. The Federal Reserve’s own review of historical approaches to monetary policy draws the broader lesson that maintaining a nominal anchor like gold does **not** guarantee price stability and can require sharply contractionary policy at the worst possible time.

### The reserve math problem

Any serious gold-restoration proposal has to answer one question first: **convertibility of what, at what price, and against what liabilities?** World Gold Council data show that central banks remain major gold holders, accounting for around one-fifth of all gold ever mined, and official-sector demand has remained strong. The ECB notes that central-bank gold reserves in 2024 stood close to Bretton Woods-era levels and that gold became the second-largest global reserve asset at market prices. Those facts matter, but they still do not imply that official gold stocks are remotely large enough to support broad modern money at anything like contemporary market parities.

The U.S. example is instructive. March 2026 monetary base was **$5.4586 trillion**, while official U.S. gold holdings totaled about **261.5 million fine troy ounces** across Fort Knox, Denver, West Point, Treasury working stocks, and Federal Reserve locations. That yields an implied 100% cover price of roughly **$20,900 per ounce** for the base alone. Since the Federal Reserve notes that U.S. gold stock is still officially valued for accounting purposes at **$42.22 per fine troy ounce**, it is clear that a modern gold restoration would require a radical monetary reset rather than a nostalgic return to previous legal parities. Covering broader money aggregates would require a substantially higher price again.

This arithmetic generalizes. Countries with **large gold stocks, small banking systems, and existing public trust** could in theory create a narrow gold-convertible base-money regime. Countries with **small gold stocks and large bank deposit liabilities** would instead face a harsh menu: buy gold at crisis prices, contract money sharply, adopt only partial backing, or suspend convertibility whenever demand for redemption surges. That last response is historically common—and exactly what destroys the credibility a gold standard is supposed to create.

### Convertibility, banking, and price stability

Restoring gold is not just about reserves; it is about **redemption mechanics**. The first practical danger is that announcing future convertibility can induce the very run it is meant to stop. The Roosevelt episode is the classic negative example: as the banking crisis worsened, the United States suspended gold in 1933 rather than defend the previous parity, because hoarding and reserve pressure were already intensifying the collapse. Likewise, Bretton Woods ended when dollar holders increasingly doubted future convertibility and a gold run loomed.

The second danger is that bank-based gold systems are not pure gold systems. Even under gold, modern economies use layered monies: deposits, bills, repos, and other claims. Recent Bank of England and BIS analyses of digital money use different terminology, but the underlying principle is old: a monetary system needs **par convertibility among different money forms**, elastic settlement capacity, and confidence that settlement assets are unquestionably redeemable. Gold convertibility does not eliminate this layering problem; it relocates it. In fact, inside-money growth on top of scarce gold can make systemic crises worse when redemption pressure arrives.

The third danger is that the gold standard’s celebrated “price stability” is the wrong stability metric for a collapse transition. Gold may help with **long-run nominal anchoring**, but recent Fed work also shows that short-run inflation or deflation under gold can be substantial and is driven by external shocks and gold flows. In a post-collapse economy, the first goal is usually not long-run average price stability; it is to stop payment breakdowns, preserve minimum credit intermediation, and avoid mass interruption of wages, taxes, and essential imports. Gold is usually a poor first-response tool for those tasks.

### Why re-adoption is unlikely to last

A modern restoration would have to satisfy simultaneously: credible reserve cover, functioning convertibility, banking-sector survivability, legal clarity, and international acceptance. That is a very high bar. History suggests that countries routinely suspend gold in war or crisis, and mass politics ultimately override metallic discipline when unemployment and bank failures become intolerable. Today’s democracies are even less likely to accept large deflations or repeated domestic austerity merely to defend a fixed metallic parity.

That does not mean gold has no role. It means the most plausible gold role after fiat collapse is **not** a universal classical gold standard. It is a narrower role as a **reserve buffer, balance-sheet repair asset, collateral base, and confidence signal**, perhaps combined with a high one-time revaluation or with limited base-money convertibility during a transition. That is very different from saying that gold would replace fiat as the main everyday money of a modern economy.

## Transition Mechanics in a Collapse

If fiat credibility collapses, the first problem is **not** how to pick a philosophically perfect monetary system. It is how to prevent the payments system and bank liabilities from disintegrating over a matter of days. IMF crisis databases show that systemic banking crises commonly involve bank holidays or deposit freezes, extraordinary liquidity support, guarantees, nationalizations, and restructuring. Capital controls are also widely used in crisis, although IMF evidence suggests that controls introduced in the middle of a crisis are often weak and hard to identify as successful, while pre-existing control frameworks offer more shielding.

That crisis-management reality has two implications for a gold restoration. First, no government could credibly jump straight from fiat breakdown to broad, on-demand metallic convertibility without first stabilizing the banking system. Second, any transition regime would almost certainly rely on **temporary administrative measures**—withdrawal limits, emergency guarantees, payment prioritization, and perhaps external financing—before any durable anchor is chosen. In other words, actual transition mechanics look more like bank-resolution law than like a textbook on metallic money.

Legal and tax issues are equally central. The U.S. Gold Clause Cases show how deeply contract law can be disrupted when the unit of account changes or gold promises are overridden. More generally, IMF work on CBDC emphasizes that replacement monies require explicit legal provisions for settlement finality, convertibility, ownership transfer, and compensation mechanisms; IMF-FSB crypto policy work also emphasizes the need for **unambiguous tax treatment**. The general lesson is broader than digital assets: no replacement money can function well if using it creates pervasive uncertainty over taxes, contract settlement, or the hierarchy of claims.

Country differences matter here. Domestic-law wage contracts, bank deposits, and taxes can usually be rewritten faster than cross-border obligations, but the precise treatment depends on each jurisdiction’s constitutional law, central-bank statutes, insolvency framework, and the governing law of private contracts. Because those legal parameters are not specified in the prompt, the safest conclusion is that **redenomination risk would be one of the central frictions in any real transition**, regardless of whether the target regime were gold, dollarization, or re-founded fiat.

### Most plausible transition sequence

The most plausible transition logic reflects crisis-management evidence, historical gold suspensions, and modern legal/payment-system design concerns.

* **Phase 1 — Emergency weekend measures**

  + Bank holiday or withdrawal limits
  + Liquidity support and deposit guarantees
  + Payment-system continuity and cash access
* **Phase 2 — Balance-sheet triage**

  + Assess bank solvency
  + Ring-fence insured deposits
  + Identify resolution candidates
* **Phase 3 — Choose interim nominal anchor** (four main candidates)

  + Re-founded rules-based fiat
  + Hard external anchor or currency board
  + Gold-linked or commodity-linked base money
  + Parallel private digital monies under regulation
* **Phase 4 — Legal and contract reset**

  + Redenominate taxes, wages, deposits, and domestic contracts
  + Resolve insolvent banks and recapitalize survivors
* **Phase 5 — Infrastructure rebuild**

  + Rebuild settlement architecture
  + Phase in durability rules and institutional reform
* **Phase 6 — Long-run regime review** (over several years)

In practice, the key sequencing question is whether convertibility comes **before** or **after** bank restructuring. History strongly suggests it must come after. Announcing hard convertibility first invites runs; restoring credibility after liabilities have already been stabilized is more plausible. That point applies not only to gold, but also to stablecoins, external pegs, and new digital sovereign money.

## Plausible Post-Fiat Alternatives

### Commodity-backed baskets and hard external anchors

A gold standard is only one kind of hard anchor. A broader **commodity basket** could, in theory, reduce the single-commodity shock problem that plagues pure gold. The classic “commodity reserve currency” idea was designed precisely to diversify relative-price risk across several widely used commodities rather than tie the unit of account to one metal. In historical model comparisons, Bordo and co-authors found that Irving Fisher’s “compensated dollar” or price-level-oriented anchor could outperform gold on uncertainty reduction by a wide margin. The basic intuition is that if the objective is price stability for goods and services, anchoring to a **basket or target path** can be superior to anchoring to a single mined commodity.

A more practical hard-anchor route in some countries would be a **currency board or foreign-currency adoption**. IMF work notes that exchange-rate anchors can be compatible with inflation objectives if reserves are adequate, fiscal dominance is absent, and policy design is coherent. But the same IMF literature on full dollarization stresses the standard trade-off: lower inflationary discretion comes at the cost of **lost monetary sovereignty, lost seigniorage, and weaker lender-of-last-resort capacity**. These systems are therefore plausible especially in small, open, already-dollarized economies—not as a universal global solution.

The IMF’s **SDR** is best understood as a proof that basket-based reserve units can exist at scale, but also a proof of their limits. The SDR is an international reserve asset defined by a basket of major currencies, but the IMF is explicit that it is **not a currency**. That means SDR-style systems are more plausible as reserve and invoicing tools than as immediate retail replacements for fiat money.

### Rules-based fiat

Among all realistic successors, the strongest candidate is a **rules-based fiat regime** rebuilt around stronger credibility than today’s discretionary system. Inflation targeting has become a durable global framework, and BIS work describes it as a form of **constrained discretion** rather than unconstrained central planning. Federal Reserve and IMF work also show why it spread so widely: a numerical target, transparency, accountability, and broad information use can stabilize expectations without relying on commodity convertibility.

This family of regimes is broader than standard inflation targeting. It includes **price-level targeting, nominal GDP-level targeting, or compensated-dollar-style rules**. The strongest case for such systems after fiat collapse is transition feasibility: they preserve the state’s capacity to supply liquidity, recapitalize banks, and support payment continuity while still binding future discretion more tightly than today’s regimes do. The trade-off is governance: these systems work only if fiscal and monetary institutions are credibly rebuilt.

### Sovereign digital money and CBDC

CBDC is not a standalone answer to fiat collapse, but it is one of the most plausible **delivery mechanisms** for a rebuilt sovereign money regime. The World Bank defines CBDC as a digital central-bank liability that can serve as medium of exchange, store of value, settlement asset, and unit of account; the BIS reports that 91% of surveyed central banks were exploring retail or wholesale CBDC in 2024, with wholesale projects generally further advanced. That tells us that governments increasingly see public digital money as part of the future base layer of the system.

The practical appeal of CBDC in a collapse scenario is that it can support **payment resilience and targeted transfers**. The World Bank explicitly notes that CBDC could be especially useful in emergencies, including rapid transfers to households and businesses. IMF work also finds that retail CBDC can coexist with existing money if calibrated carefully, although it may still affect funding structures and requires safeguards such as holding limits or macroprudential tools. The Bank of England’s work on a digital pound makes the same broader point: any such system would require legislation and an extended design phase, meaning it is most realistic as part of a **stabilization-and-rebuild strategy**, not as an overnight replacement.

### Stablecoins, private currencies, narrow banking, and decentralized crypto

Official institutions are increasingly willing to separate **payment innovation** from **monetary sovereignty**. That is the crucial issue with private currencies. IMF work on digital money acknowledges that e-money and stablecoins can improve convenience and competition. But BIS and Bank of England work are far more cautious about their systemic role. The Bank of England argues that systemic stablecoins must be fully anchored and exchangeable at par with existing forms of money, while its later regulatory work prefers full backing with **central-bank deposits** precisely to minimize run risk and preserve “singleness of money.”

Stablecoins are therefore plausible as **regulated payment instruments**, but weak candidates for the core monetary standard unless they are effectively transformed into a tightly supervised extension of public money. IMF and BIS analysis emphasizes that payment stablecoins remain vulnerable to runs and that reserve disclosure can itself create unstable equilibria depending on beliefs about reserve quality. That does not eliminate their usefulness; it means their stable operation at systemic scale depends on public regulation, safe backing assets, and settlement rules that look increasingly like public money by another name.

**Decentralized cryptocurrencies** remain the least plausible candidates for replacing fiat at state scale. The Basel Committee states plainly that crypto-assets do not reliably perform the standard functions of money and are unsafe to rely on as medium of exchange or store of value. ECB work on Bitcoin trading also points toward globally synchronized speculative dynamics rather than maturing monetary use. IMF-FSB policy work therefore frames crypto primarily as a challenge to macro-financial stability and monetary sovereignty rather than a ready-made successor regime.

**Narrow banking** is more serious. The IMF’s Chicago Plan revisitation argues that fully reserve-backed deposits could eliminate bank runs if monetary liabilities are fully backed by government money and credit is funded through non-run-prone liabilities. The trade-off is that such a system changes the structure of credit creation and may move more intermediation toward capital markets, equity, and nonbank finance. Narrow banking is thus one of the strongest options for the **financial stability** objective, but not a costless way to preserve today’s credit model.

### Best-case, most-likely, and worst-case paths

The **best-case** path is not a pure gold restoration. It is a rapid crisis stabilization in which the state preserves payments, ring-fences insured deposits, recapitalizes key banks, and re-establishes a credible nominal anchor within months. In this path, governments may use gold reserves as collateral, balance-sheet support, or a confidence signal, but the enduring regime is more likely to be **rules-based fiat**, perhaps temporarily supported by an exchange-rate anchor or a limited commodity signal. Payment modernization—potentially including wholesale CBDC or tokenized public settlement assets—arrives over the next two to five years rather than overnight.

The **most-likely** path after a genuinely severe fiat collapse is a **hybrid transition**. Some countries would experience partial foreign-currency substitution; some would adopt hard pegs or currency boards; some larger states would attempt re-founded fiat with stronger fiscal and monetary rules. Gold would rise in reserve portfolios and private balance sheets, but formal gold convertibility would be narrow, partial, or deferred. The institutional end-state would probably combine public money, tightly regulated private payment media, and more conservative bank liability structures.

The **worst-case** path is long disorder. In that scenario, bank holidays, emergency controls, and contract rewrites proliferate; multiple monies circulate in parallel; legal uncertainty over redenomination undermines finance; and political pressure repeatedly destroys each attempted peg or metallic promise. In such environments, gold and foreign cash often circulate informally, but that does **not** amount to a functioning gold standard. Instead, it reflects the absence of a trusted public unit of account.

## Recommendations and Sources

### Policy recommendations for governments

Governments should prepare for crisis transitions **before** a crisis by writing down a legal order of operations for bank resolution, deposit protection, emergency liquidity authority, capital-flow management, contract redenomination, and tax treatment of replacement monies. The most serious failures in monetary collapse are usually failures of sequencing and law, not failures of theory.

They should also avoid making gold-convertibility promises they cannot support with reserves and bank restructuring. If governments want gold to play a role, the defensible roles are reserve management, collateral, and confidence signaling—not broad retail convertibility on day one. Promising too much convertibility too early is the shortest path to a run.

Finally, governments should distinguish between **anchors** and **infrastructure**. A CBDC or tokenized public-money rail can improve resilience and emergency transfers, but it will not substitute for fiscal credibility, central-bank legislation, and a coherent nominal anchor.

### Policy recommendations for central banks

Central banks should maintain **contingency playbooks** for several post-collapse possibilities rather than one preferred ideology: re-founded inflation or price-level targeting, temporary exchange-rate anchors, currency-board-style arrangements, wholesale CBDC or tokenized reserve architectures, and collateralized emergency payment continuity. History suggests that institutions need optionality more than doctrinal purity.

They should also design digital public money to **coexist** with bank money rather than displace it abruptly. IMF work suggests holding limits, fee structures, and macroprudential safeguards are important to contain destabilizing shifts out of bank deposits. That advice is especially relevant in collapse transitions, when the temptation to flee deposits into any safer-looking instrument is highest.

Reserve management should remain diversified. Central banks have been increasing gold reserves precisely because gold remains free of default risk and useful as a hedge in some reserve portfolios. That supports holding gold as a confidence asset—even while rejecting the stronger claim that gold should again become the sole monetary standard.

### Policy recommendations for investors

For investors, the highest-confidence implication is that a true fiat-collapse transition would likely involve **liquidity stress, legal uncertainty, and multiple competing monies** before any new stable regime emerges. The most robust posture is therefore diversification across jurisdictions, custody arrangements, and claim types, with emphasis on assets whose legal status is clear and whose settlement does not depend on fragile intermediaries.

Gold remains a plausible hedge in such environments because official institutions continue to hold it for safety, liquidity, and return characteristics, and BIS work finds it can serve as a hedge in some reserve portfolios. But investors should not confuse “gold as hedge” with “gold standard imminent.” The former is plausible; the latter is much less so.

Unregulated stablecoins and decentralized crypto should be treated cautiously in collapse scenarios. Official work consistently highlights stablecoin run risk and crypto’s failure, so far, to meet the standard functions of money at systemic scale. That does not eliminate tactical use cases, especially in cross-border or parallel-market settings, but it makes them weak candidates for a core wealth-preservation strategy unless legal and reserve transparency are exceptionally strong.

### Open questions and limitations

The prompt does not specify the **speed of collapse**, the **geographic scope**, the **survival of state capacity**, or whether the hypothetical shift is coordinated internationally or fragmented by country. Those variables could materially change the ordering of alternatives. A small open economy with credible access to a foreign anchor faces a different menu from a reserve-currency issuer with very large offshore liabilities. Likewise, a collapse limited to one currency area produces very different transition mechanics from a simultaneous multilateral breakdown.

A second limitation is that no single internationally comparable money aggregate cleanly answers the reserve-cover question across countries. For that reason, the report uses a **U.S. illustrative calculation** for scale and relies otherwise on official reserve and historical evidence rather than forcing a false-precision global cover ratio.
