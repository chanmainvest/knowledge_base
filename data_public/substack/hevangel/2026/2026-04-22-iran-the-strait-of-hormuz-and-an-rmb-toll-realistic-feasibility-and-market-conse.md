# Iran, the Strait of Hormuz, and an RMB Toll: Realistic Feasibility and Market Consequences

## Executive Summary

Iran **cannot lawfully impose a generalized “toll” for mere transit** through the Strait of Hormuz under the widely accepted *transit passage* framework for international straits.

In the UN Convention on the Law of the Sea (UNCLOS), all ships enjoy a right of transit passage “which shall not be impeded,” and “there shall be no suspension of transit passage.” In addition, UNCLOS separately bars charges “by reason only of … passage through the territorial sea,” allowing charges only for “specific services rendered.” A broad, mandatory toll for transit would therefore be treated by most states as **inconsistent with the legal regime** that protects chokepoint navigation.

That said, **legal illegitimacy does not preclude coercive enforcement**. Iran may be able to create a *de facto* toll in practice—especially during heightened conflict—by:

* Threatening or attacking non‑compliant shipping
* Offering a “safe passage corridor” or “clearance” process that ships comply with to reduce risk

A March 2026 U.S. Congressional Research Service (CRS) report (republished by USNI) assessed Iran has the capacity to disrupt shipping via mines, speedboats, submarines, shore‑based cruise missiles, aircraft, and other systems—and that restoring shipping could take “days, weeks, or perhaps months,” depending on Iranian tactics.

Recent reporting also describes an IRGC “tolled passageway” attempt that (per the report) routed ships through Iranian territorial waters around Larak Island, after vessels had been favoring Omani waters.

**Requiring payment in Chinese renminbi (RMB) is mechanically feasible but strategically fragile**. Payments could be routed via *offshore RMB (CNH)* and/or China’s Cross‑Border Interbank Payment System (CIPS), which is built to clear/settle cross‑border RMB payments.

Yet the global financial system remains deeply dollar‑centric across reserves, invoicing, and FX liquidity:

* Global FX turnover reached **$9.6 trillion/day** in April 2025, with USD on one side of **89.2%** of trades; RMB’s share was **8.5%**.
* Official reserves totaled **$13.14 trillion** in 2025Q4; USD’s share was **56.77%** and RMB’s share **1.95%**.

**Bottom line:** an RMB‑denominated toll—if attempted—would most likely:

* **Raise short‑term energy risk premia** and intermittently disrupt shipping
* Produce **symbolic “de‑dollarization” headlines**
* Have **limited direct impact** on USD reserve status and global settlement architecture unless it becomes a wider, durable shift in *oil pricing/invoicing* backed by sustained Chinese institutional support

The largest market impacts would come from **physical disruption risk** (oil/LNG supply, insurance, freight), not from the currency denomination of the toll itself.

## What Is at Stake in Hormuz—and Why a “Toll” Matters

The Strait of Hormuz is an unusually high‑impact chokepoint because it concentrates both **flow volume** and **limited bypass capacity**.

### Oil and Refined Products

* EIA estimates oil flow through Hormuz averaged **20 million barrels/day in 2024**
* This represents about **20% of global petroleum liquids consumption**
* Hormuz volumes were **more than one‑quarter of global seaborne oil trade**

The IEA similarly states that roughly **20 mb/d** transited in 2025 and that around **25% of the world’s seaborne oil trade** passes through the Strait, with bypass options limited.

### LNG

* EIA estimates **~20% of global LNG trade** transited Hormuz in 2024
* Qatar exported about **9.3 Bcf/d**, and the UAE **0.7 Bcf/d**, through Hormuz

The IEA notes a closure would strand exports from Qatar and the UAE representing about **19% of global LNG trade**.

### Geography and Traffic Lanes

* The Strait is only **29 nautical miles** wide at its narrowest
* It uses **two 2‑mile shipping channels** plus a **2‑mile buffer**

Maritime reporting indicates that at the narrowest point, both inbound and outbound channels can lie in **Omani waters**, and recent traffic has attempted to avoid Iranian waters when risk rises—underscoring that Iran’s “jurisdictional” control is incomplete even though its **military reach** can extend across the Strait.

**Implication:** any credible “toll” regime is less about lawful taxation and more about **coercive leverage** over a critical artery.

## Feasibility of an RMB Toll

### International Law and Iran’s Treaty Posture

Under UNCLOS Part III (straits used for international navigation):

* **Transit passage must not be impeded**
* **Transit passage cannot be suspended**

This is the core legal obstacle to a unilateral, mandatory toll regime.

Iran **signed UNCLOS on 10 December 1982** but has **not ratified** it, making it a signatory but not a party.

Iran also filed interpretive declarations at signature arguing that certain UNCLOS provisions—including **transit passage**—were “quid pro quo” treaty bargains that only **states parties** should benefit from.

Most maritime powers nonetheless treat transit passage as a foundational rule of navigation, reflected in freedom‑of‑navigation practice. A toll would therefore likely be framed internationally as **unlawful interference** with navigation even if Iran claims non‑party status.

---

### Practical Enforcement: What Iran Can and Cannot Control

A realistic “toll” requires credibility across:

* **Detection**
* **Interdiction or attack capability**
* **Willingness to absorb escalation costs**

#### Iran’s Coercive Tools

CRS assesses Iran can disrupt shipping using:

* Naval mines
* Fast attack craft
* Submarines
* Shore‑based cruise missiles
* Aircraft

CRS also notes a U.S.-led effort to restore shipping could take **days to months**, depending on Iranian tactics.

#### Limits on Jurisdictional Control

* Shipping lanes can be routed to minimize time in Iranian waters
* During elevated risk, traffic has deliberately stayed within **Omani waters**

A toll framed as a coastal‑state regulatory measure would therefore be hard to apply universally without becoming a **threat‑based regime**.

#### Likely Enforcement Modality

USNI reporting describes an IRGC attempt to create a “tollbooth” passageway routing ships through Iranian waters near Larak Island—effectively making the toll route “safer” than the alternative.

#### Expected Responses

A toll that impedes transit passage would likely trigger:

* Expanded naval escort operations and ISR
* Mine countermeasures
* Intensified sanctions and diplomatic pressure

Markets would focus on whether coalitions can **reduce the risk premium** faster than Iran can **reimpose it**. CRS explicitly warns that prolonged disruption could produce unprecedented oil‑market conditions.

## How Iran Could Demand RMB: Channels and Constraints

“Demanding RMB” involves several possible mechanisms, each constrained by compliance risk.

### Direct RMB Settlement (CNH / CIPS)

* CIPS clears and settles cross‑border RMB payments
* 2025 volume: **180 trillion RMB**
* Participants: **193 direct**, **1,573 indirect** (early April 2026)

CIPS still interfaces with SWIFT messaging and remains far smaller in reach.

### Bilateral China–Iran Oil-for-RMB Structures

* China is estimated to buy **~90% of Iran’s exported oil**
* Iran could link “safe passage” to RMB‑denominated offtake and Chinese‑linked shipping or insurance

### Escrow or “Clearance” Models

* Security deposits tied to passage clearance
* Main constraint: **bank willingness and sanctions exposure**

### Barter and Non‑Bank Settlement

* Barter, offsets, or commodity swaps
* Scales poorly for global shipping but viable for selective corridors

**Key constraint:** compliance sensitivity. A universal RMB‑only toll would likely be limited to gray‑zone operators.

### Scenario Table (Converted from Table Format)

#### Best Case (35%)

* **Action:** Toll announced, then abandoned
* **Enforcement:** Low
* **RMB feasibility:** Low
* **Timeframe:** Days to weeks
* **Market impact:** Risk premium fades; oil and gas partially retrace

#### Most Likely (50%)

* **Action:** Selective coercive corridor with sporadic incidents

* **RMB feasibility:** Medium for subset of actors
* **Timeframe:** Weeks to months

#### Worst Case (15%)

* **Action:** Sustained de facto control; quasi‑regular toll
* **Enforcement:** Medium disruption, low legitimacy
* **RMB feasibility:** Medium with Chinese facilitation
* **Timeframe:** Months or longer
* **Market impact:** Persistent energy shock; trade‑finance stress; inflation risk

## Quantitative Market Impacts

### Scale of Potential Toll Flows

* Annual Hormuz oil flow: **~7.3 billion barrels**
* Hypothetical $1/bbl toll:

  + **$7.3 billion/year**
  + **~$20 million/day**

Comparison:

* Global FX turnover: **$9.6 trillion/day**
* Official FX reserves: **$13.14 trillion**

**Conclusion:** mechanically negligible for FX markets and reserve composition.

---

### Settlement Systems

* **SWIFT:** dominant global messaging layer
* **CIPS:** RMB settlement system, interoperable with SWIFT
* **USD rails:** CHIPS (~~$1.8T/day) and Fedwire (~~$4T/day)

Even enforced RMB settlement would **reroute marginal flows**, not replace dollar infrastructure.

---

### Oil Invoicing vs Settlement

* Oil benchmarks remain USD‑quoted
* BIS analysis: commodities are predominantly priced in USD

Settlement in RMB does not equal RMB price discovery without deep derivatives markets and balance‑sheet acceptance.

---

### Reserve Currency Implications

* 2025Q4 COFER:

  + USD: **56.77%**
  + RMB: **1.95%**

IMF analysis shows diversification has not translated into large RMB gains, and RMB reserve share has stalled post‑2022.

The most plausible channel is **political reaction**, not mechanical flow.

### Trade Finance

## Assumptions and Unspecified Variables

* **Toll scale:** Per‑voyage vs per‑barrel matters materially
* **Duration:** Volatility vs regime shift hinges on persistence
* **China’s role:** Banking and clearing support is decisive
* **Sanctions response:** De‑risking speed constrains feasibility

**Core conclusion:** an RMB toll is best understood as a **selective coercion and sanctions‑evasion tool**, not a credible mechanism for rapid displacement of the USD, whose dominance rests on deep liquidity, reserve holdings, and sticky invoicing patterns far larger than any plausible toll flow.
