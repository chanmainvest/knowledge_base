## **Executive summary**

Uranium markets entered 2026 with prices still far above the 2020–2022 range but below the early‑2024 spike: uranium spot was reported around **$89.40/lb (Feb 20, 2026)** by TradingEconomics, after a **> $100/lb** episode in early 2024. The sector’s equity narrative remains anchored on a structural need to rebuild primary supply (new mines + restarts) into a demand backdrop shaped by life extensions, restarts, and new-build momentum, with utilities increasingly focused on security of supply.

A key “load-bearing” fundamental is the persistent gap between **mine output** and **reactor requirements**. World Nuclear Association (WNA) reports **2024 mine production of 60,213 tU**, describing that as **~90% of world reactor requirements** (implying requirements in the high‑60k tU range). WNA also provides a **2025 uranium requirement** figure of **68,920 tU**. This supply gap has historically been filled by inventories and secondary supplies, but WNA notes “finite secondary supplies” and provides region-level inventory figures, supporting the thesis that the marginal pound increasingly needs to come from higher‑cost, longer‑cycle supply.

Policy has shifted from “nuclear permissive” to “nuclear proactive” in several jurisdictions, with the US standing out for: (1) **restricting Russian uranium products** (with waivers and quotas), and (2) **industrial-policy spending** across fuel-cycle chokepoints (enrichment, HALEU), plus strategic procurement efforts. The key investable implication is that uranium is increasingly treated as a strategic commodity (supply chain, defense, and critical minerals framing), raising the probability of state-linked demand/supply interventions that can amplify cycles.

At the equity level, the investable universe still splits into three distinct risk/return buckets:  
**large producers/integrated players** (Cameco, Kazatomprom) with earnings/cashflow visibility but contract-lag; **developers** (NexGen, Denison) that are option-like on permitting/financing/build; and **vehicles/ETFs** (SPUT, URA) that offer cleaner exposure to uranium price or a diversified equity beta—at the cost of fees, premiums/discounts, and embedded factor tilts.

## **Market overview and price dynamics**

Uranium pricing is best understood as two partially connected markets: **spot** (thin, financial flows can matter) and **term/long‑term contracting** (utility procurement, multi‑year delivery schedules). WNA emphasizes that most utility procurement is done through longer‑term contracts, with spot/short‑term a minority share of global volumes, and notes the spot market can be sensitive to incremental buyers/sellers.

### **Recent spot and term price signals**

Cameco’s uranium price page (which references industry indicators) reports **Jan 31, 2026 spot $94.28/lb** and **long‑term $89.00/lb**. TradingEconomics reported spot around **$89.40/lb on Feb 20, 2026**. A key contextual anchor for “recent trends” is the early‑2024 spike: a Wall Street Journal report on Kazatomprom cited a move from **~$43/lb at the start of 2023** to a **~$106/lb level in early 2024**, and another WSJ item noted prices peaked near **$107/lb** and then fell to **$87.75/lb by end‑Q1 2024**.

These points highlight a classic commodity pattern: a sharp repricing (tightness narrative), a mean-reversion phase (profit-taking, risk-off, marginal supply response), and then a stabilization at a higher trading range—consistent with supply’s long lead times and demand’s “must-run baseload” characteristics. WNA’s “uranium markets” discussion and company commentary both reinforce the lead-time/inventory realities that slow supply response.

### **Key drivers behind price behavior**

Contracting cycles matter more than daily spot prints for producer cashflows. Cameco explicitly highlights that utilities are seeking dependable supply as secondary supplies thin and new production faces longer lead times and inflation pressures—factors that support term contracting. In the US, EIA data shows that utilities still have meaningful uncovered requirements: for 2024–2034, EIA reported utilities had **184 million lb U₃O₈e of unfilled requirements**, while contracts in place could deliver up to **234 million lb U₃O₈e** over that period. Even though this is US-only, it matters because US utilities are both a major demand center and the jurisdiction most actively reshaping fuel policy.

A second driver is the increasing interaction between **financial uranium holding vehicles** and the physical market. SPUT’s own materials describe its purpose as holding uranium (U₃O₈) in an exchange-traded structure, which can concentrate investment demand into physical.

## **Supply-demand fundamentals and geopolitics**

### **Primary mine production vs reactor requirements**

WNA reports **2024 world mine production of 60,213 tU**, and states this is **~90% of world reactor requirements**. A separate WNA briefing states **~67,000 tU** is required each year to fuel world nuclear power plants. WNA also reports a **2025 uranium requirement** of **68,920 tU**.

This implies the market relies structurally on **secondary supplies** (inventories, underfeeding/overfeeding dynamics in enrichment, reprocessed uranium, tails, etc.) and that the “call on new mines” intensifies if demand grows. WNA’s supply discussion explicitly describes the role of secondary supplies and inventories, while also indicating that secondary sources are finite.

### **Secondary supplies and inventories**

WNA provides region-level uranium inventory figures (from its Nuclear Fuel Report): at end‑2024, inventories are cited at **~42,000 tU (USA)**, **~40,000 tU (Europe)**, and **~65,000 tU (East Asia)**. Inventories reduce near-term scarcity risk, but the strategic question is *who holds them* and *whether they are willing to sell* in a geopolitical environment that increasingly penalizes dependence on adversarial supply. That creates the possibility of “inventory hoarding” instead of “inventory smoothing,” which can steepen upcycles.

### **Reactor demand, builds, and retirements**

As of mid‑February 2026, WNA reports **437 operable nuclear power reactors** and **74 under construction** globally, alongside its uranium requirement estimates. While the report focus here is uranium, the “new build” pipeline matters because it converts policy into long-lived demand, but only after long development cycles—meaning uranium mining investment must lead reactor load by years. WNA and IAEA messaging emphasize the need for timely investments across the fuel cycle to support nuclear growth objectives.

### **Geopolitical supply concentration and disruption risk**

Uranium supply is concentrated both geographically and in specific production methods. Kazakhstan remains dominant, and its production is ISR-heavy; this helps cost competitiveness but can create **single-point vulnerabilities** (reagent availability, logistics corridors, policy direction). Kazatomprom’s disclosures repeatedly flag **sulphuric acid availability** as a constraint, and their 2026 guidance is explicitly subject to acid availability.

Geopolitics matters not only for uranium mining, but also for conversion/enrichment and shipping corridors. Kazatomprom’s 2024 results discussion describes export routing considerations (including via Russia and via Trans-Caspian alternatives) and provides contextual detail on US/Russia uranium trade restrictions and retaliation measures, highlighting that nuclear fuel is now part of broader sanctions dynamics.

## **Policy, regulation, and strategic stockpiles**

### **US policy: Russian uranium restrictions**

The US enacted restrictions on Russian uranium products with a structure that includes **prohibitions plus waiver/quota mechanics**. The US Nuclear Regulatory Commission’s fact sheet explains that the Russia uranium import ban affects **unirradiated low-enriched uranium (LEU) and natural uranium** produced in Russia, and outlines that waivers can apply through a defined period. The Federal Register notice documents the Department of Energy’s actions to implement the statute, including annual waiver limits for 2024–2027.

Market implication: even “temporary” waivers can tighten procurement planning because utilities must secure compliant supply chains, often via longer-term contracts—supportive for producers with restart capacity and credible jurisdictional profiles.

### **US policy: fuel-cycle industrial policy and HALEU/enrichment**

The policy tailwind is not only about uranium mining; it’s also about the **fuel-cycle bottlenecks** that set the realizable demand for uranium (conversion/enrichment capacity). In January 2026, the US DOE announced **$2.7B** in awards to restore domestic uranium enrichment capability (including HALEU), explicitly framing it as part of reducing reliance on Russia. Specific awards included major funding for Centrus and others to expand enrichment capacity.

### **Strategic stockpiles and government procurement**

The US has pursued direct procurement of uranium for strategic purposes. As an example, Energy Fuels announced a contract with DOE to sell uranium concentrates for the **US Uranium Reserve** program. (Multiple US suppliers have announced similar arrangements in prior years, reflecting the re-emergence of state demand as a market participant.)

### **Europe and other jurisdictions**

Europe’s sustainable finance treatment of nuclear remains a policy lever. The EU’s taxonomy approach has included nuclear under specific conditions (contested politically and legally), but the overall direction has been to make nuclear easier to finance in “net zero” frameworks than in the post‑Fukushima period.

## **Equity and vehicle analysis**

### **How to read uranium equities**

Uranium equities are not a single factor bet. They blend: (1) uranium price sensitivity, (2) contracting strategy and lag effects, (3) jurisdiction/regulatory risk, (4) restart/build execution, and (5) investor-flows beta (ETFs, retail cycles). Cameco’s disclosures show how long-term contracts, market-related pricing mechanisms, and inventory/purchases are used to manage delivery risk, which can dampen or delay spot sensitivity.

### **Portfolio allocation suggestions by risk profile**

These are **framework allocations** (not personalized advice). The key idea is to match *instrument structure* to *risk tolerance*.

Conservative: emphasize diversified or contract-cushioned exposure—core positions in **URA** (broad equity beta) and **large producers** (Cameco, Kazatomprom), optionally complemented by a smaller **SPUT** allocation for direct uranium beta. URA’s scale and fee level are explicitly disclosed by Global X, and SPUT’s premium/discount and NAV tracking are disclosed by Sprott.

Moderate: barbell approach—core in **Cameco/Kazatomprom/URA**, plus selected developers (**NexGen, Denison**) where permitting/engineering milestones are tangible (CNSC decision path; Denison engineering/procurement progress and liquidity).

Aggressive: overweight developers and higher operational leverage producers (**Paladin, Boss**) where small changes in price/execution can drive large equity moves—while keeping an anchor in producers/ETF to mitigate single-asset risk. Paladin’s realized price/cost structure and Boss’s quarterly production trajectory illustrate the operational leverage profile.
