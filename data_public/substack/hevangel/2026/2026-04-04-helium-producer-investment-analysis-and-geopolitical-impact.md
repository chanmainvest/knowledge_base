## Executive summary

Helium is a strategic, non‑renewable industrial commodity with no substitute in ultra‑low‑temperature cryogenics (below roughly −256 °C, i.e., −429 °F). USGS emphasizes that nothing substitutes for helium in cryogenic applications requiring these temperatures, while some substitution is possible in welding, lifting gas, and certain diving mixes.

The market’s defining feature is supply concentration. Using USGS world production data (2023), the United States (including withdrawals from Cliffside) and Qatar together represent the overwhelming majority of global production, with Russia, Algeria, Canada, and a handful of smaller producers comprising the remainder.

Pricing is not transparently quoted like oil or copper. Government and industry observers note helium transactions are commonly governed by confidential, bilateral contracts; the USGS publishes an estimated “base price” for private Grade‑A helium, and also reports surcharges were common during tight periods.

Over the last five full years (2021–2025), the USGS estimated base price for Grade‑A helium rose from $7.57/m³ (2021) to $11/m³ (2022) and $14/m³ (2023–2024), then eased to $12/m³ (2025)—still materially above 2021 levels.

Geopolitically, the helium supply chain is currently exposed to Persian Gulf shock risk through two coupled channels:

* **Upstream coupling to LNG:** Qatar helium is produced from natural gas/LNG value chains; shutdowns at Ras Laffan therefore directly threaten helium output and contract fulfillment.
* **Maritime chokepoint risk:** UNCTAD reports ship transits through the Strait of Hormuz have “come to a near halt,” with ~97% drop versus pre‑conflict averages—disrupting logistics, insurance, and freight economics.

For investors, this implies helium exposure splits into two “styles”:

* **Defensive / quality exposure:** via the global industrial gas oligopoly (Linde, Air Liquide, Air Products), where helium is a small revenue line but a strategic product embedded in long‑term contracts and high switching costs.
* **High beta / scarcity optionality:** via smaller helium-focused producers and developers (e.g., Renergen, Royal Helium, Desert Mountain Energy, Pulsar), which offer more direct leverage to supply tightness but carry execution, financing, and concentration risk.

## Global supply–demand overview

### Production sources and supply concentration

USGS world production estimates (million m³ of helium) illustrate extreme concentration.

* **2023 (world total ~176 million m³):**

  + Qatar: ~66
  + United States: ~64 extracted from natural gas + ~18 from Cliffside
  + Remaining supply: Russia, Algeria, Canada, others
* **2024e (world total ~180 million m³):**

  + Qatar: ~64
  + United States: ~68 extracted + ~13 from Cliffside
  + Russia: ~17
* **2025e (world total ~190 million m³):**

  + New operations and storage additions noted
  + Country-level splits show internal inconsistencies in USGS tables; 2024e data is more reliable for allocation analysis

Practical portfolio implication: helium is concentrated not only by country, but by **mega‑facilities and hubs**:

* Wyoming/LaBarge–Shute Creek (U.S.)
* Ras Laffan (Qatar)
* Amur (Russia)

Outages at any of these create **outsized price and allocation impacts**.

## Recent capacity and operational changes

USGS notes the following developments in 2025:

* Six new U.S. helium operations began producing:

  + Three in New Mexico
  + One each in Colorado, Kansas, and Montana
* A helium storage cavern came online in Beaumont, Texas, capable of storing excess production
* New helium facilities began operations:

  + One in Canada
  + One in South Africa

These additions **do not eliminate concentration risk**, but they can reduce the severity of shortages when logistics remain functional.

Russia remains a swing factor:

* The Amur Gas Processing Plant restart has been uneven
* Shipments began from at least one train in 2023
* Additional ramp‑ups have been reported but remain vulnerable to geopolitics and logistics

## Inventories and the post‑Federal Helium Reserve era

The transition of the U.S. Federal Helium System significantly reduces shock absorption.

* ~85.7 million m³ remaining (FY2021)
* ~60.7 million m³ remaining (FY2022)
* ~51.5 million m³ remaining (FY2023)
* Assets transferred from BLM to GSA for disposal
* Federal Helium System assets sold in two lots and transferred June 27, 2024

**Investment relevance:** the market has moved from a state‑buffered model to a **fully commercial, contract‑driven market**—raising sensitivity to outages and chokepoint disruptions.

Healthcare and research demand is **structurally inelastic**. Mitigation focuses on recycling and closed‑loop systems rather than substitution.

## Price trends (last five years)

USGS estimated base prices for private Grade‑A helium:

* 2021: $7.57/m³
* 2022: $11/m³
* 2023: $14/m³
* 2024: $14/m³
* 2025: $12/m³ (often with surcharges)

Interpretation: even after easing, pricing remains structurally higher than pre‑shortage levels. USITC documents **four major helium shortages** since ~2005.

## Iran conflict scenario analysis

### Why Iran conflict matters disproportionately

Key risk amplification factors:

* Qatar produced ~66 million m³ in 2023 (high‑30% global share)
* Ras Laffan shutdowns and force majeure impair helium quickly
* Strait of Hormuz traffic down ~97%
* ACS reports ~⅓ of world helium supply affected
* Disruptions lasting >2 weeks can take months to normalize

### Supply disruption scenarios (table converted to bullets)

**Short disruption**

* Description: Limited outage; partial shipping normalization
* Supply effect: Temporary Qatar interruption; alternative sourcing
* Time to normalize: 1–2 months
* Probability: ~30%
* Market impact: Surcharges, priority allocations

**Medium disruption**

* Description: Sustained corridor impairment
* Supply effect: Persistent logistics constraints
* Time to normalize: 2–6 months
* Probability: ~45%
* Market impact: Allocations, spot price spikes

**Prolonged disruption**

* Description: Extended conflict and closures
* Supply effect: Structural shortage
* Time to normalize: 6–12+ months
* Probability: ~25%
* Market impact: Severe rationing, contract repricing

## Closing investment perspective

Helium combines **extreme supply concentration, opaque pricing, limited substitution, and high geopolitical sensitivity**. For investors, exposure ranges from defensive industrial gas leaders to high‑beta scarcity plays—each requiring clear alignment with risk tolerance, capital discipline, and geopolitical awareness.
