## Executive summary

Hedge funds are usually **private “pooled” investment vehicles** whose shares are not sold to the general public, and the regulatory focus is often on the **manager/adviser** and on **how the fund trades** rather than on the fund being a “retail product.” In the U.S., for example, the SEC explains that private funds are not registered as investment companies, cannot publicly offer their securities, and are structured to fit exclusions such as **Investment Company Act sections 3(c)(1) or 3(c)(7)**.

Across the U.S., EU, and UK, hedge fund “risk compliance” is best understood as a **stack of guardrails** that:

* Limit who can invest
* Require baseline governance and surveillance
* Constrain leverage and short selling (directly in EU/UK, indirectly in the U.S.)
* Prohibit market abuse such as insider trading and manipulation

EU law explicitly requires AIFMs to separate risk management from portfolio management, run stress tests, set maximum leverage, and implement liquidity risk management systems for most funds.

A key update for U.S. readers: although the SEC adopted sweeping private fund adviser rules in August 2023, the SEC confirmed that a **June 5, 2024 Fifth Circuit decision vacated those rules**. As a result, the 2023 private‑fund‑specific requirements are **not currently in effect**, and compliance reverts to existing Advisers Act rules (anti‑fraud, custody, compliance program, books and records).

What makes hedge funds feel “different” from retail trading is not special legal privilege but **institutional plumbing and mandatory processes**:

* Multiple compliance checks before orders are sent
* Ongoing risk monitoring
* Prime broker margining
* Independent valuation practices
* Trade surveillance

Retail traders can legally adopt many hedge‑fund‑like techniques (position sizing, pre‑trade discipline, liquidity awareness) without hedge fund infrastructure. The main boundaries remain legal and ethical: avoid material nonpublic information, manipulation, and misunderstandings of margin and short-selling mechanics.

## Regulatory rules and agencies that govern hedge fund risk compliance

A useful mental model: regulation hits hedge funds in **three layers**:

* Fund offering rules
* Manager/adviser rules
* Market and trading integrity rules

### U.S. overview (SEC, CFTC, FinCEN, FINRA, Federal Reserve)

In the U.S., hedge funds are typically “private funds,” structured under **3(c)(1)** or **3(c)(7)** of the Investment Company Act. Investor eligibility is enforced through **accredited investor** and **qualified purchaser** definitions (Reg D Rule 501(a)).

The **manager** (often an SEC‑registered investment adviser) is the primary regulated entity. Advisers must:

* Maintain a compliance program (Rule 206(4)‑7)
* Appoint a Chief Compliance Officer
* Review policies annually
* Uphold fiduciary duties to clients

### Key reporting regimes (U.S.)

* **Form PF (confidential)**: Required for advisers with ≥$150M in private fund AUM to support systemic‑risk oversight
* **Form 13F (public)**: Required for managers with ≥$100M discretion in 13(f) securities

### Short selling (U.S.)

Under Regulation SHO:

* Orders must be properly marked
* Brokers must locate borrow availability
* Failures to deliver must be closed out
* Rule 201 restricts shorting after a 10% intraday decline

### Insider trading, leverage, and AML (U.S.)

* Insider trading enforced primarily through Rule 10b‑5 and Rule 10b5‑1
* Leverage is constrained indirectly via broker margin rules (Reg T, FINRA)
* Prime brokers act as critical leverage and risk gatekeepers
* FinCEN’s August 2024 rule expanded AML/CFT obligations to certain advisers

### Important U.S. status update

The SEC confirms the 2023 Private Fund Adviser rules were **vacated**. However:

* Core safeguards (custody rule, audits, fiduciary duty) remain intact
* Audited pooled vehicles distributing financials within 120 days remain compliant under custody frameworks

---

### EU overview (AIFMD, MAR, SSR)

In the EU, hedge funds are regulated as **Alternative Investment Funds (AIFs)**, with heavy emphasis on **manager governance**.

Key AIFMD requirements include:

* Separation of risk management and portfolio management
* Maximum leverage limits per fund
* Liquidity management systems and stress testing
* Transaction-level reconstructability
* Independent valuation governance
* Depositary oversight and conflict management
* Investor disclosures about leverage, liquidity, and prime brokers
* Regulatory reporting via **Annex IV / Article 24**

**Short selling (EU SSR):**

* Reporting when net shorts exceed 0.2%
* Public disclosure at 0.5%
* Ban on uncovered short sales unless settlement is assured

**Market abuse (EU MAR):**

* Prohibits insider dealing, unlawful disclosure, manipulation
* Requires detection and reporting of suspicious orders “without delay”

---

### UK overview (FCA, UK MAR, UK SSR reforms)

The UK retains and adapts EU‑derived frameworks:

* **UK MAR** governs market abuse
* **SYSC** mandates systems and controls against financial crime
* **AIFMD‑style reporting (Annex IV)** remains in place
* UK Short Selling Regulation is being restructured under 2025 legislation, replacing assimilated EU SSR

## Internal risk compliance controls hedge funds typically use

Think of hedge fund compliance as a **factory quality‑control system**: designed to prevent illegal trades, operational errors, and catastrophic losses.

### Institutional trade lifecycle (flowchart converted to bullets)

* **Trade idea**
* **Pre‑trade compliance checks**

  + Restricted list / MNPI screening
  + Short‑sale locate verification
  + Market abuse red‑flag checks
* **Pre‑trade risk checks**

  + Position and leverage limits
  + Liquidity constraints
  + Counterparty exposure limits
* **Order routing and execution**
* **Post‑trade controls**

  + Trade reconciliation and confirmations
  + Trade surveillance reviews
* **Ongoing monitoring and escalation**

  + Stress tests and scenario analysis
  + Valuation governance
* **Investor and regulator reporting**

---

### Explanation of key controls (unchanged content, reformatted)

* **Risk limits**: Caps on exposure, leverage, concentration to prevent amplified losses
* **Pre‑trade controls**: MNPI blocks, borrow verification, liquidity checks
* **Post‑trade monitoring**: Reconciliations, error detection, auditability
* **Counterparty limits & margining**: Managed exposure to prime brokers and dealers
* **Liquidity management**: Align redemption terms with asset liquidity
* **Stress testing**: Survival analysis under adverse but plausible scenarios
* **Valuation policies**: Independent and impartial pricing oversight
* **Conflicts controls**: Personal trading rules, side‑by‑side allocation safeguards
* **Trade surveillance**: Detection of manipulation or suspicious patterns
* **Recordkeeping**: Orders, communications, valuations, and reports retained

## How compliance makes hedge funds behave differently from retail traders

Hedge funds operate inside a **risk‑and‑control machine**, not because they trade faster, but because they:

* Manage third‑party capital
* Use institutional leverage
* Face multi‑dimensional regulatory expectations

The shift to **T+1 settlement** in U.S. markets (May 28, 2024) further pressures institutions toward automation and clean operations.

---

## Hedge fund vs retail trading (table converted to bullets)

### Speed and automation

* **Hedge funds**: OMS/EMS, automated checks, straight‑through processing
* **Retail**: Mostly manual via broker platforms

### Pre‑trade checks

* **Hedge funds**: Formal compliance gates (MNPI, leverage, liquidity, locate)
* **Retail**: Broker‑enforced rules plus self‑discipline

### Access to leverage

* **Hedge funds**: Prime brokers and derivatives
* **Retail**: Broker margin, Reg T, PDT constraints

### Short selling

* **Hedge funds**: Institutional borrow infrastructure
* **Retail**: Broker availability, HTB risk

### Liquidity management

* **Hedge funds**: Fund‑level liquidity frameworks
* **Retail**: Often informal, learned during volatility

### Counterparty risk

* **Hedge funds**: Multiple counterparties, exposure limits
* **Retail**: Usually one broker

### Post‑trade monitoring

* **Hedge funds**: Independent reconciliation and surveillance
* **Retail**: Broker statements and personal review

### Oversight

* **Hedge funds**: PM, risk, compliance, ops, auditors, custodians
* **Retail**: Self‑oversight

### Reporting

* **Hedge funds**: Form PF, 13F, Annex IV
* **Retail**: None (but anti‑fraud laws apply)

## Practical, legal ways retail traders can adopt hedge‑fund habits

* Use a **risk budget**, not just a trade idea
* Define a single **loss number** before entry
* Prefer **limit orders** in thin liquidity
* Understand **settlement timing** under T+1
* Avoid **accidental leverage traps**
* Maintain a personal **restricted list** if your job creates MNPI risk

---

## Risks and legal boundaries for retail traders

Retail traders are subject to the **same core market‑integrity rules**:

* No insider trading (MNPI)
* No manipulation or deceptive schemes
* Compliance with short‑selling rules
* Awareness of licensing/AML obligations if trading for others
