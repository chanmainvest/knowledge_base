## Executive Summary

The center of gravity in the oil industry has moved decisively from the shareholder-owned Anglo-American majors that once dominated the concession system to state-backed national champions. OPEC’s own institutional history describes the market at its founding in 1960 as one dominated by the “Seven Sisters,” while the IEA now says the large international majors own **less than 13%** of global oil and gas production and reserves; by contrast, national oil companies own **more than half of production** and **close to 60% of reserves**. That ownership shift is the single most important fact behind the decline of the original Seven Sisters and the rise of the so-called New Seven Sisters.

The original Seven Sisters were not simply large firms; they were the institutional architecture of the postwar oil order. Through concession agreements, control of transportation and refining, and coordination mechanisms that dated back to interwar arrangements such as Achnacarry, they shaped supply, pricing, and access to reserves. Their position weakened first through producer-state bargaining, then through OPEC coordination, then through outright nationalizations and participation agreements in the 1960s and 1970s. By the late 1990s and early 2000s, the “seven” had effectively collapsed into a much smaller set of supermajors through mergers such as BP–Amoco, Exxon–Mobil, and Chevron–Texaco.

Carola Hoyos’s “New Seven Sisters” label, introduced in the *Financial Times* in 2007, captured the new hierarchy: Saudi Aramco, Gazprom, CNPC, NIOC, PDVSA, Petrobras, and Petronas. The point of the label was not that these firms were operationally identical to the old cartel, but that reserve power and state backing had become more important than private shareholding and downstream integration alone. Today, that logic remains broadly intact, even if the group itself is uneven in governance, sanctions exposure, and disclosure quality.

Among listed and transparently reported majors, Saudi Aramco remains the standout on reserves, production, profitability, and market value. ExxonMobil and Chevron are the strongest-positioned U.S. integrated majors after the Pioneer and Hess transactions, respectively. Shell remains a powerful integrated IOC with a particularly strong LNG and trading franchise, while BP has become the clearest example of a major recalibrating its transition strategy toward cash-flow resilience. Petrobras remains strategically important because of its giant Brazilian pre-salt base, even though its disclosure set is not as easily comparable field-for-field to the Western supermajors.

A practical conclusion follows. If the question is **who owns the molecules**, the answer is still mostly NOCs. If the question is **who converts portfolio quality, technology, trading, and capital discipline into equity value**, the leading IOCs still matter enormously. The modern industry is therefore best understood as a two-tier system: reserve-heavy state champions on one side, and capital-efficient, technologically advanced, publicly traded integrators on the other.

## From Concession Cartel to State-Backed Oil Order

The original Seven Sisters emerged from an era in which private international companies gained long-duration concessions over producing territories, especially in the Middle East. Their power rested on three reinforcing capabilities: control of upstream concessions; ownership of shipping, refining, and marketing assets; and the ability to coordinate market behavior. OPEC’s official history explicitly says the international market was dominated by the Seven Sisters before the organization’s founding, while U.S. diplomatic records on the 1954 Iranian settlement show how an international consortium of major firms was designed to restore Iranian oil exports after nationalization.

That order weakened because producer states learned to bargain collectively and then to nationalize. OPEC was created in Baghdad in September 1960 by Iran, Iraq, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia, and Venezuela precisely during a period of decolonization and political transition. By 1972, Iraq nationalized the Iraq Petroleum Company’s assets, and U.S. records from the same period show that producer governments were also forcing participation agreements on Gulf concessions, shifting equity and control from the companies to the states.

By the time the dust settled, the old Seven Sisters had lost the strategic asset that mattered most: direct control over giant low-cost reserves. What survived was not the old system but a narrower class of supermajors that remained formidable in deepwater, LNG, technology, finance, project execution, and trading. The mergers of the late 1990s and early 2000s were therefore not just consolidation for scale; they were a response to the fact that the old reserve-access model had ended. Britannica’s corporate histories and company records trace that consolidation through BP’s acquisition of Amoco, Exxon’s merger with Mobil, and Chevron’s merger with Texaco.

## The Original Seven Sisters

The canonical Seven Sisters are usually identified as **Anglo-Iranian/Anglo-Persian**, **Gulf Oil**, **Royal Dutch/Shell**, **Standard Oil of California**, **Standard Oil of New Jersey**, **Standard Oil of New York**, and **Texaco**. Enrico Mattei of Eni popularized the label as a critique of their dominance over world oil. The lineage of several of these firms is clear in current corporate histories: Anglo-Persian became Anglo-Iranian and then BP; Standard Oil of California evolved into Chevron; Standard Oil of New Jersey became Exxon; and Standard Oil of New York became Mobil, later part of ExxonMobil.

### Members, roles, and present descendants

**Anglo-Persian / Anglo-Iranian**

* Historical role: British-backed concession holder in Persia/Iran; foundational Middle East producer
* Main present-day lineage: BP

**Gulf Oil**

* Historical role: Major integrated U.S. multinational with strong refining/marketing position
* Main present-day lineage: Largely absorbed into Chevron; brand survives separately in some markets

**Royal Dutch/Shell**

* Historical role: Anglo-Dutch integrated major; global shipping, refining, and trading strength
* Main present-day lineage: Shell plc

**Standard Oil of California**

* Historical role: Major U.S. upstream and downstream player; central in Saudi development through SoCal/CASOC
* Main present-day lineage: Chevron

**Standard Oil of New Jersey**

* Historical role: Largest Standard Oil successor; global integrated system
* Main present-day lineage: Exxon, later ExxonMobil

**Standard Oil of New York**

* Historical role: Major Standard successor; global marketing and refining footprint
* Main present-day lineage: Mobil, later ExxonMobil

**Texaco**

* Historical role: Large U.S. integrated company; key partner in Saudi and global operations
* Main present-day lineage: Chevron after 2001 merger

### Why they declined

The Seven Sisters declined because the structure that made them powerful became politically untenable. The key inflection points were Iranian nationalization and the 1954 consortium compromise; OPEC’s founding in 1960; producer-state participation demands in the Gulf; and waves of nationalization, especially Iraq’s 1972 move against IPC. These developments transferred rent, then equity, then strategic control to host governments.

The final step in the decline was corporate recombination. By the early 2000s, the old seven no longer existed as seven separate strategic actors. BP absorbed Amoco and other assets, Exxon and Mobil recombined, and Chevron absorbed Texaco. In other words, the Seven Sisters did not disappear because oil stopped mattering; they disappeared because producer states captured the reserve base, leaving the private firms to consolidate around technology, integration, and capital markets.

## The New Seven Sisters

The “New Seven Sisters” term is generally traced to Carola Hoyos’s 2007 *Financial Times* framing of the giant state-backed firms that had eclipsed Western rivals in reserve access and political weight. The commonly cited group is **Saudi Aramco**, **Gazprom**, **CNPC**, **NIOC**, **PDVSA**, **Petrobras**, and **Petronas**.

The analytical point of the phrase still holds. These companies were not merely large producers; they were instruments of state policy, fiscal extraction, industrial strategy, and geopolitical influence. The IEA’s current framing now gives that thesis quantitative backing: the large international majors own less than 13% of global production and reserves, while NOCs own more than half of production and close to 60% of reserves. In that sense, the New Seven Sisters represent the post-concession order in which sovereign control over resources matters more than global brand or private ownership alone.

### Members and roles

**Saudi Aramco** — Saudi Arabia

* Core role: World-leading reserve holder and low-cost upstream producer; increasingly active in gas, chemicals, LNG, and Asian downstream markets

**Gazprom** — Russia

* Core role: State gas champion with major export and geopolitical leverage; more gas-weighted than oil-centric

**CNPC** — China

* Core role: State upstream champion and international resource-acquisition vehicle; central to China’s energy security strategy

**NIOC** — Iran

* Core role: Reserve-rich national champion constrained by sanctions and geopolitics

**PDVSA** — Venezuela

* Core role: Historically a major export NOC anchored in giant heavy-oil resources

**Petrobras** — Brazil

* Core role: Deepwater and pre-salt specialist with material state ownership and strong reserve growth in the Santos Basin

**Petronas** — Malaysia

* Core role: Highly internationalized state energy group with major LNG reach

The key difference between the old and new “sisters” is not just ownership. The old Seven Sisters built power through private control of concessions and vertically integrated logistics; the new group’s power comes from sovereign title to reserves, domestic policy backing, and privileged access to national balance sheets. That is why the modern IOC–NOC relationship is more interdependent than hierarchical: the majors often bring technology, trading, and execution; the NOCs bring reserves, host-country authority, and fiscal endurance.

### What the peer comparison shows

Saudi Aramco is in a separate league on reserve depth and upstream scale. Its reserve base of roughly **250 billion boe** dwarfs the reserve bases disclosed by the listed IOCs in the source set gathered here, and its 2024 net income of **$106.2 billion** remained extraordinary even after the decline from 2023.

Among the listed IOCs, ExxonMobil and Chevron look the most advantaged on North American unconventional scale and portfolio quality after the Pioneer and Hess transactions. Exxon’s 2024 production of **4.3 mmboe/d** and proved reserve base of **19.9 bn boe** put it clearly ahead of the Western integrated peer group in the source set, while Chevron’s Hess acquisition materially strengthened its future Guyana exposure.

Shell remains strategically distinctive because of LNG, trading, and system integration. Even where its reserve figure was not directly extractable from the gathered sources, its **2.836 mmboe/d** of 2024 production and its continuing reshaping of the North Sea and power portfolio show a company leaning into integration rather than pure reserve accumulation. BP, by contrast, is the clearest case of a major reprioritizing near-term resilience and capital discipline while still preserving a lower-carbon growth platform.

Petrobras is important for a different reason: it is a bridge case between a reserve-rich NOC and a listed capital-market company. Its official 2024 reserve statement shows **11.4 bn boe** of proved reserves and attributes reserve additions chiefly to the Brazilian pre-salt, especially the Santos Basin core fields.

## Analytical Conclusions

The Seven Sisters mattered because they controlled access. The New Seven Sisters matter because states control title. That sounds simple, but it changes nearly everything: fiscal terms, sanction exposure, investment cycles, the role of sovereign wealth, and the bargaining power of downstream and consuming states. The old order was private and concessionary; the current order is sovereign and negotiated.

That does **not** mean the IOCs are strategically irrelevant. In fact, the present market assigns high value to exactly the capabilities states cannot always replicate internally: shale execution, deepwater project delivery, LNG portfolio optimization, trading, chemicals integration, and disciplined capital returns. ExxonMobil’s and Chevron’s latest mergers show that the Western majors are doubling down on advantaged barrels rather than trying to recreate the old concession system. Shell’s portfolio behavior points in the same direction, though with greater emphasis on LNG, power, and trading.

The transition strategies disclosed by the majors also reveal a widening strategic dispersion. ExxonMobil has committed up to **$30 billion** to lower-emission investments during **2025–2030** and links those investments to CCS, hydrogen, lower-emission fuels, and lithium. Chevron is funding lower-carbon businesses more cautiously, but with an explicit cumulative spending frame and operating-intensity targets. Shell is advancing decarbonization targets and systems integration, while BP’s 2024–2025 posture shows a sharper rebalancing toward cash-generative hydrocarbons and selective low-carbon bets. Aramco, meanwhile, frames transition activity around operational emissions, CCS, gas growth, and selective “new energies,” but without the same kind of granular low-carbon capex breakout seen at some Western peers.

The result is an industry that is more concentrated in reserves than it is in equity-market value. Aramco remains the clearest example of that divergence: it combines the scale of a sovereign reserve holder with the valuation visibility of a listed company. The Western majors, by contrast, command much smaller reserve positions but continue to trade on their ability to generate free cash flow, execute mega-projects, and reposition portfolios faster than many state incumbents.

## Open Questions and Limitations

This report prioritizes official and primary sources, but not every requested figure was directly retrievable from the source set collected here. In particular, some 2024 **revenue**, **reserves**, and **low-carbon capex** figures for Shell, bp, Petrobras, and several opaque NOCs were not directly extractable from official summary pages or snippets, so they are marked **unspecified** rather than estimated.

The present-day comparison should therefore be read as a **high-confidence disclosed subset**, not as a fully exhaustive global league table. The biggest limitation is comparability across ownership models: listed IOCs usually provide cleaner SEC/20-F-style disclosures than wholly state-owned firms such as CNPC, NIOC, PDVSA, QatarEnergy, ADNOC, or Petronas. That disclosure asymmetry is itself analytically important, because it reinforces the difference between reserve power and investable transparency in today’s oil market.
